## 2. Executive Concept Summaries

### Module A: Electromagnetism (Electrostatics & Magnetostatics)

#### A1. Electrostatics: Fields, Dipoles, Conductors & Gauss's Law
1. **Coulomb's Law & Electrostatic Force:**
   - Force between point charges in vacuum: $\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{12}$.
   - Small angle suspended charges: When two identical charges $q$ of mass $m$ hang on threads of length $L$, equilibrium separation $x$ with $\tan\theta \approx \sin\theta = \frac{x}{2L}$ gives $F_e = m g \tan\theta \implies \frac{q^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 x^2} = m g \frac{x}{2L} \implies x = \left(\frac{q^2 L}{2\pi \epsilon_0 m g}\right)^{1/3}$.
2. **Electric Dipole Dynamics:**
   - **Electric Dipole Moment:** $\vec{p} = q(2\vec{a})$, directed from the negative charge $-q$ to the positive charge $+q$.
   - **Torque in Uniform Field:** $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$, magnitude $\tau = p E \sin\theta$. Maximum torque occurs at $\theta = 90^\circ$; stable equilibrium at $\theta = 0^\circ$; unstable equilibrium at $\theta = 180^\circ$.
   - **Potential Energy:** $U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -p E \cos\theta$. Work required to rotate from $\theta_1$ to $\theta_2$ is $W_{\text{ext}} = \Delta U = -p E(\cos\theta_2 - \cos\theta_1)$.
3. **Conductors at Electrostatic Equilibrium:**
   - Excess charge resides strictly on the outer conductor surface (due to mutual Coulomb repulsion).
   - Electric field inside the bulk of a conductor is identically zero: $\vec{E}_{\text{in}} = 0$.
   - Electrostatic potential is uniform throughout the conductor volume and equal to the surface potential ($V = \text{constant}$).
   - Electric field immediately outside the conductor surface is normal to the surface: $\vec{E} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\hat{n}$.
   - **Surface Curvature & Corona Discharge:** Local surface charge density $\sigma \propto \frac{1}{r_{\text{curvature}}}$. At sharp tips/edges ($r \to 0$), $\sigma$ and $E$ become extremely high ($> 3\times 10^6\text{ V/m}$), causing local ionization/breakdown of surrounding air molecules. Opposite ions are neutralized while like ions are repelled, forming an "electric wind" that rapidly carries charge away. Technological uses: lightning arrestors, Van de Graaff generators, electrostatic precipitators (industrial smoke cleanup), electrostatic paint spraying.
4. **Gauss's Theorem & High-Symmetry Charge Configurations:**
   - Statement: $\Phi_E = \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0}$.
   - **Long Straight Cylindrical Conductor (Radius $a$, Linear Density $\lambda$):**
     - Outside ($r \ge a$): Gaussian cylinder of radius $r$, length $l$: $E(2\pi r l) = \frac{\lambda l}{\epsilon_0} \implies E(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r}$.
     - Inside Conducting Wire ($r < a$): All charge on surface $\implies Q_{\text{enc}} = 0 \implies E(r) = 0$.
     - Inside Non-Conducting Solid Cylinder with Uniform $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\pi a^2}$: $Q_{\text{enc}} = \rho (\pi r^2 l) = \lambda l \frac{r^2}{a^2} \implies E(2\pi r l) = \frac{\lambda l r^2}{\epsilon_0 a^2} \implies E(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 a^2} r$ ($E \propto r$).
   - **Orbiting Electron Dynamics:** An electron in circular orbit of radius $r$ around a positively charged wire experiences centripetal force provided by electrostatic attraction: $F_c = \frac{m_e v^2}{r} = e E(r) = \frac{e \lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r} \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{e\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 m_e}}$. Tangential speed is independent of orbital radius $r$; angular velocity $\omega = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{1}{r}\sqrt{\frac{e\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 m_e}}$; orbital period $T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi r \sqrt{\frac{2\pi \epsilon_0 m_e}{e\lambda}} \propto r$.
5. **Capacitance & Partially Filled Dielectrics:**
   - Isolated parallel plate capacitor of area $A$, separation $d$, charge $q_0 = C_0 V_0$ where $C_0 = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}$.
   - Insertion of dielectric slab of thickness $b < d$ and dielectric constant $\kappa$:
     - Electric field in air gap: $E_0 = \frac{q_0}{\epsilon_0 A} = \frac{V_0}{d}$.
     - Electric field in dielectric: $E_1 = \frac{E_0}{\kappa}$.
     - Potential difference: $V = E_0(d - b) + E_1 b = E_0 \left[(d - b) + \frac{b}{\kappa}\right] < V_0$.
     - Resulting capacitance: $C = \frac{q_0}{V} = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{(d - b) + b/\kappa} > C_0$.

#### A2. Magnetostatics: Biot-Savart Law, Ampère's Law & Magnetic Forces
1. **Biot-Savart Law:**
   - Fundamental differential equation: $d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I d\vec{\ell} \times \hat{r}}{r^2} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I d\vec{\ell} \times \vec{r}}{r^3}$.
   - **Center of Circular Current Loop (Radius $a$):** $d\vec{\ell} \perp \hat{r} \implies B = \oint \frac{\mu_0 I dl}{4\pi a^2} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a^2} (2\pi a) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a}$.
   - **Point on the Axis of Circular Loop (Axial Distance $x$):** Perpendicular components cancel by symmetry; axial components sum to:
     $$B_x = \oint dB \cos\phi = \frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2(a^2 + x^2)^{3/2}}$$
     - At center ($x=0$): $B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a}$.
     - Far field ($x \gg a$): $B \approx \frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2x^3} = \frac{\mu_0 (I \pi a^2)}{2\pi x^3} = \frac{\mu_0 \mu_m}{2\pi x^3}$ (magnetic dipole field).
   - **Finite Straight Conductor:**
     $$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a}(\sin\phi_1 + \sin\phi_2)$$
     - Infinitely long wire ($\phi_1 = \phi_2 = \pi/2$): $B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}$.
     - Semi-infinite wire ($\phi_1 = \pi/2, \phi_2 = 0$): $B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a}$.
2. **Ampère's Circuital Law:**
   - Vector integral form: $\oint_{\mathcal{C}} \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{\ell} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$.
   - **Thick Cylindrical Conductor (Radius $a$) with Non-Uniform Current Density:**
     - For $J(r) = J_0\left(1 - \frac{r}{a}\right)$:
       - Total current: $I = \int_0^a J(r) 2\pi r dr = 2\pi J_0 \left[\frac{a^2}{2} - \frac{a^3}{3a}\right] = \frac{\pi J_0 a^2}{3} \implies J_0 = \frac{3I}{\pi a^2}$.
       - Inside wire ($r < a$): $I_{\text{enc}}(r) = 2\pi J_0 \left[\frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^3}{3a}\right] = \pi J_0 r^2 \left(1 - \frac{2r}{3a}\right)$.
       - Ampère's law: $B(r) \cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}(r) \implies B(r) = \frac{\mu_0 J_0 r}{2}\left(1 - \frac{2r}{3a}\right) = \frac{3\mu_0 I r}{2\pi a^2}\left(1 - \frac{2r}{3a}\right)$.
       - Outside wire ($r \ge a$): $I_{\text{enc}} = I \implies B(r) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$.
     - For $J(r) = k r$:
       - $I_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r (k r') 2\pi r' dr' = \frac{2}{3}\pi k r^3 \implies B(r) = \frac{1}{3}\mu_0 k r^2$.
       - Outside: $B(r) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = \frac{\mu_0 k a^3}{3r}$.
3. **Lorentz Force & Particle Trajectories:**
   - Total force: $\vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B})$.
   - **Zero Work Theorem:** The magnetic force $\vec{F}_B = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$ is always strictly perpendicular to instantaneous velocity $\vec{v}$ (since $(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \cdot \vec{v} = 0$). Power delivered is $P = \vec{F}_B \cdot \vec{v} = 0$, so work done $W = \int P dt = 0$. Magnetic fields change particle direction, NEVER speed or kinetic energy.
   - **Circular Motion & Cyclotron Resonance:** Magnetic force provides centripetal acceleration: $q v B = \frac{m v^2}{R} \implies R = \frac{m v}{q B}$.
     - Period: $T = \frac{2\pi R}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{q B}$ (independent of speed $v$ and radius $R$).
     - Cyclotron frequency: $f_c = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{q B}{2\pi m}$.
     - Maximum kinetic energy upon reaching Dee radius $R_D$: $K_{\max} = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\max}^2 = \frac{q^2 B^2 R_D^2}{2m}$.
   - **Relativistic Breakdown in Cyclotrons:** As particle energy approaches relativistic regimes, inertial mass increases as $m = \gamma m_0 = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$. The orbital frequency $f = \frac{q B}{2\pi \gamma m_0}$ decreases with increasing energy, causing particles to arrive late at the Dee gap, falling out of phase with the fixed-frequency RF voltage and ceasing to accelerate. Solution: Synchrocyclotrons (frequency modulated) or Isochronous cyclotrons (radially increasing magnetic field).
4. **Forces on Conductors & Magnetic Dipole Moment:**
   - Magnetic force on wire element: $d\vec{F} = I(d\vec{\ell} \times \vec{B})$.
   - Parallel conductors: Force per unit length between parallel currents separated by $d$ is $\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}$ (attractive for parallel currents, repulsive for antiparallel).
   - Magnetic dipole moment of planar loop: $\vec{\mu} = I \vec{A}$.
   - Revolving electron in Bohr orbit: current $I = \frac{e}{T} = \frac{e v}{2\pi r}$, loop area $A = \pi r^2 \implies \mu_L = I A = \frac{e v r}{2}$. Since angular momentum is $L = m_e v r$, $\vec{\mu}_L = -\frac{e}{2m_e} \vec{L}$.
   - Quantized magnetic moment: Substituting $L = n\hbar$ gives $\mu = n \left(\frac{e\hbar}{2m_e}\right) = n \mu_B$, where $\mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} \approx 9.274 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J/T}$ is the **Bohr Magneton**.

### Module B: Modern & Nuclear Physics

#### B1. Thermal Radiation & Early Quantum Theory
1. **Blackbody Radiation Laws:**
   - **Stefan-Boltzmann Law:** Total emissive power of a blackbody: $P = \epsilon \sigma A T^4$. For an ideal blackbody, emissivity $\epsilon = 1$. Net power exchanged with surroundings at $T_0$: $P_{\text{net}} = \epsilon \sigma A (T^4 - T_0^4)$.
   - **Wien's Displacement Law:** Peak emission wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature: $\lambda_m T = C_W$, where $C_W = 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}\cdot\text{K}$ (or $2.90 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}\cdot\text{K}$).
   - **Spectral Characteristics:** As $T$ increases, total radiated energy increases dramatically ($\propto T^4$), and the peak wavelength $\lambda_m$ shifts toward shorter wavelengths (higher photon energies).
2. **Breakdown of Classical Physics (Ultraviolet Catastrophe):**
   - Classical Rayleigh-Jeans formula treated cavity radiation as continuous standing waves with equipartition theorem energy ($k_B T$ per mode):
     $$I(\nu, T)d\nu = \frac{8\pi \nu^2}{c^3} k_B T d\nu$$
   - As frequency $\nu \to \infty$ (short wavelength UV regime), $I(\nu) \to \infty$, which violates the conservation of energy and contradicts experimental spectra.
3. **Planck's Quantum Hypothesis (1900):**
   - Cavity atomic oscillators cannot absorb or emit energy continuously, but only in discrete bundles (quanta): $E_n = n h \nu$, where $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$.
   - Planck's radiation law perfectly fits experimental data across all wavelengths:
     $$I(\nu, T) = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2} \frac{1}{\exp\left(\frac{h\nu}{k_B T}\right) - 1}$$

#### B2. Photoelectric Effect & Compton Scattering
1. **Einstein's Photoelectric Equation (1905):**
   - Light delivers energy in localized quanta called photons: $E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$.
   - Conservation of energy during absorption of a single photon by a surface electron:
     $$K_{\max} = hf - \Phi = \frac{hc}{\lambda} - \Phi = e V_s$$
     where $\Phi$ is the work function (minimum energy required to liberate an electron), and $V_s$ is the stopping potential.
   - Threshold frequency $f_0 = \frac{\Phi}{h}$; Cut-off wavelength $\lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{\Phi}$. For $\lambda > \lambda_0$, no photoelectrons are emitted regardless of beam intensity.
   - Photocurrent is strictly proportional to incident light intensity ($I_{\text{photo}} = \eta \frac{P \lambda}{hc} e$), whereas electron maximum kinetic energy depends solely on light frequency $f$.
2. **Compton Scattering (1923):**
   - Scattering of high-energy photons (X-rays, $\gamma$-rays) by stationary, loosely bound electrons in matter, resulting in scattered photons of longer wavelength $\lambda'$.
   - **Compton Shift Formula:**
     $$\Delta \lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_e c}(1 - \cos\theta) = \lambda_c (1 - \cos\theta)$$
     where $\lambda_c = \frac{h}{m_e c} \approx 2.426 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m} = 0.002426\text{ nm}$ is the Compton wavelength of the electron, and $\theta$ is the photon scattering angle.
   - **Core Assumptions:**
     1. Radiation consists of relativistic corpuscular photons with $E = h\nu$ and $p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{E}{c}$.
     2. The target electron is initially stationary and quasi-free (binding energy $E_B \ll E_{\text{photon}}$).
     3. The collision is completely elastic, strictly obeying relativistic conservation of energy and linear momentum.
   - **Origin of the Unmodified Line (Thomson Line, $\Delta \lambda = 0$):** When photons collide with tightly bound inner-shell electrons, the entire atom (mass $M_{\text{atom}} \approx 10^4 - 10^5 m_e$) recoils as a unit. Substituting $M_{\text{atom}}$ into the Compton shift equation yields $\Delta \lambda = \frac{h}{M_{\text{atom}} c}(1 - \cos\theta) \approx 0$.
   - Maximum shift occurs at direct backscattering $\theta = 180^\circ$: $\Delta \lambda_{\max} = 2\lambda_c \approx 4.85 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m} = 4.85\text{ pm}$.

#### B3. Wave-Particle Duality & Atomic Structure
1. **de Broglie Matter Wave Hypothesis (1924):**
   - Any moving particle with momentum $p = mv$ possesses an associated matter wavelength:
     $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}$$
   - For an electron accelerated from rest across potential difference $V$:
     $$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_e e V}} = \frac{1.226}{\sqrt{V}}\text{ nm}$$
   - Experimental validation: Davisson-Germer and G.P. Thomson observed electron diffraction through nickel and thin gold foils, confirming wave nature.
2. **Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle (1927):**
   - Conjugate variable limits: $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$ and $\Delta E \cdot \Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$.
   - Proves electrons cannot reside permanently inside the atomic nucleus (nuclear confinement $\Delta x \approx 10^{-14}\text{ m} \implies \Delta p \approx 5\times 10^{-21}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s} \implies K_e > 20\text{ MeV}$, which far exceeds observed $\beta$-decay energies).
3. **Bohr Atomic Model of Hydrogen & Hydrogen-like Ions ($Z$):**
   - **Postulates:**
     1. Electrons move in stable circular orbits under Coulomb force without emitting electromagnetic radiation.
     2. **Quantization of Angular Momentum:** $L = m_e v r = n\hbar = n \frac{h}{2\pi}$ ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$).
     3. **Quantum Jumps:** Radiation of frequency $f = \frac{E_i - E_f}{h}$ is emitted/absorbed when an electron transitions between energy states.
   - **Derivation of Orbital Radius:** Centripetal force = Coulomb force:
     $$\frac{m_e v^2}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_0} \frac{Z e^2}{r^2} \implies v = \frac{n\hbar}{m_e r} \implies r_n = \frac{4\pi \epsilon_0 \hbar^2}{m_e Z e^2} n^2 = \frac{n^2}{Z} a_0$$
     where Bohr radius $a_0 = \frac{4\pi \epsilon_0 \hbar^2}{m_e e^2} \approx 0.0529\text{ nm} = 0.529\text{ \AA}$.
   - **Derivation of Quantized Energy Levels:**
     $$E_n = K + U = \frac{1}{2} m_e v^2 - \frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_0} \frac{Z e^2}{r} = -\frac{1}{8\pi \epsilon_0} \frac{Z e^2}{r_n} = -\frac{m_e Z^2 e^4}{32\pi^2 \epsilon_0^2 \hbar^2} \frac{1}{n^2} = -13.6 \frac{Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV}$$
   - **Rydberg Transition Formula:**
     $$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_\infty Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right) \quad \text{where } R_\infty = \frac{m_e e^4}{8\epsilon_0^2 h^3 c} \approx 1.097 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}$$
   - **Pickering Series in $\text{He}^+$ ($Z=2$):** Transitions ending at $n_f = 4$:
     $$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_\infty (2^2) \left(\frac{1}{4^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right) = R_\infty \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{(n_i/2)^2}\right)$$
     When $n_i$ is even ($n_i = 2m$), the lines coincide exactly with the Balmer series of Hydrogen ($n_f = 2, n_i = m$). When $n_i$ is odd, intermediate spectral lines appear, historically misidentified as stellar hydrogen lines by Edward Pickering in 1896.
   - **Six Limitations of the Bohr Model:**
     1. Applicable only to single-electron systems ($\text{H}, \text{He}^+, \text{Li}^{2+}$); fails for multi-electron atoms.
     2. Violates Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle by assigning simultaneous definite orbits and momenta.
     3. Cannot explain fine-structure splitting of spectral lines (relativistic spin-orbit coupling).
     4. Cannot explain the Zeeman Effect (line splitting in magnetic fields) or Stark Effect (in electric fields).
     5. Fails to predict transition probabilities and relative spectral line intensities.
     6. Assumes flat 2D orbits; atoms are spherical 3D structures.

#### B4. X-Ray Generation & Spectroscopy
1. **Coolidge X-Ray Tube Operation:**
   - Electrons produced via thermionic emission from a heated tungsten filament are accelerated across a high tube potential difference $V$ ($10 - 100\text{ kV}$) toward a heavy metal anode/target (tungsten $\text{W}$ or molybdenum $\text{Mo}$) embedded in a water-cooled copper block ($> 98\%$ of energy converted to heat, $< 2\%$ to X-rays).
2. **X-Ray Spectrum Features:**
   - **Continuous Spectrum (Bremsstrahlung / Braking Radiation):** Caused by rapid deceleration of incident electrons in the Coulomb fields of heavy target nuclei.
   - **Duane-Hunt Cut-Off Rule ($\lambda_{\min}$):** An electron that surrenders all its kinetic energy in a single deceleration event creates a photon of maximum frequency and minimum wavelength:
     $$e V = h f_{\max} = \frac{h c}{\lambda_{\min}} \implies \lambda_{\min} = \frac{h c}{e V}$$
     $\lambda_{\min}$ depends solely on accelerating tube voltage $V$, independent of target material.
   - **Characteristic Spectrum ($K_\alpha, K_\beta, L_\alpha$):** Sharp, high-intensity peaks resulting from inner-shell atomic vacancies created by projectile electrons. An electron from an outer shell ($L$ or $M$) drops into the vacancy ($K$-shell), emitting a photon of energy $\Delta E = E_L - E_K$.
   - **Moseley's Law:** $\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b)$, proving atomic number $Z$ (nuclear charge) determines elemental chemical identity rather than atomic weight.
3. **Crystal Structure Analysis (Bragg's Law):**
   - High-energy X-rays have wavelengths ($\sim 0.1\text{ nm}$) comparable to atomic lattice interplanar spacings $d$. Constructive interference occurs when path difference equals an integer number of wavelengths:
     $$2d \sin\theta = n\lambda \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$

#### B5. Nuclear Physics & Radioactivity
1. **Nuclear Composition & Properties:**
   - Nucleus consists of $Z$ protons and $N = A - Z$ neutrons (nucleons).
   - Nuclear radius: $R = R_0 A^{1/3}$, where $R_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m} = 1.2\text{ fm}$. Nuclear density is roughly constant across all elements: $\rho_{\text{nuc}} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}\text{ kg/m}^3$.
   - Distance of closest approach in Rutherford $\alpha$-scattering: $\frac{1}{2} m_\alpha v^2 = \frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_0} \frac{(2e)(Ze)}{d} \implies d = \frac{4 Z e^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 m_\alpha v^2}$.
2. **Mass Defect & Binding Energy:**
   - Total mass of any stable bound nucleus is strictly less than the sum of its individual constituent free nucleons:
     $$\Delta m = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M_{\text{nucleus}}$$
   - Binding energy: $B = \Delta m \cdot c^2 = \Delta m(\text{in u}) \times 931.5\text{ MeV}$.
   - **Mean Binding Energy per Nucleon ($\bar{B} = B/A$):** Rises rapidly for light nuclei, reaches a broad maximum near $^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$ at $\approx 8.8\text{ MeV/nucleon}$, and steadily drops to $\approx 7.6\text{ MeV/nucleon}$ for heavy actinides like $^{235}\text{U}$.
     - Splitting heavy nuclei (Fission) releases energy.
     - Combining light nuclei (Fusion) releases even greater energy per unit mass.
3. **Radioactive Decay Kinetics:**
   - **Decay Law:** $-\frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N \implies N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$.
   - **Activity ($A$):** Number of disintegrations per second: $A(t) = -\frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$. SI unit: Becquerel ($1\text{ Bq} = 1\text{ decay/s}$); Historic unit: Curie ($1\text{ Ci} = 3.7 \times 10^{10}\text{ Bq}$).
   - **Half-Life ($T_{1/2}$):** Time for $N$ or $A$ to decrease by half:
     $$T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$$
   - **Carbon-14 Archaeological Dating:**
     - Formation in upper atmosphere: cosmic ray neutrons strike nitrogen: $^{14}_7\text{N} + n \to {}^{14}_6\text{C} + p$.
     - Living organisms maintain steady equilibrium ratio $^{14}\text{C}/^{12}\text{C} \approx 10^{-12}$ via respiration/photosynthesis.
     - Upon death, uptake halts and $^{14}\text{C}$ decays via $\beta^-$-decay ($^{14}_6\text{C} \to {}^{14}_7\text{N} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$) with half-life $T_{1/2} = 5730\text{ years}$.
     - Archaeological age equation:
       $$t = \frac{1}{\lambda} \ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A}\right) = \frac{T_{1/2}}{\ln 2} \ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A}\right)$$
4. **Nuclear Reactions, Q-Value & Nuclear Reactors:**
   - General reaction: $X + a \to Y + b + Q$.
   - **Q-Value:** $Q = [(m_X + m_a) - (m_Y + m_b)] \times 931.5\text{ MeV}$. If $Q > 0$, exothermic (exoergic); if $Q < 0$, endothermic (endoergic).
   - **Nuclear Fission:** Heavy nucleus splits into intermediate fragments after capturing a thermal neutron:
     $$^{235}_{92}\text{U} + n \to {}^{236}_{92}\text{U}^* \to {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3n + 173.3\text{ MeV}$$
   - **Thermal Nuclear Reactor Components:**
     - **Fuel:** Enriched $^{235}\text{U}$ ($3 - 5\%$) or natural uranium ($0.7\%\ {}^{235}\text{U}$).
     - **Moderator:** Light materials (low $A$) that slow fast fission neutrons ($\sim 2\text{ MeV}$) to thermal speeds ($\sim 0.025\text{ eV}$) via elastic collisions without absorbing them (materials: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, heavy water $\text{D}_2\text{O}$, high-purity graphite).
     - **Control Rods:** Strong neutron absorbers used to adjust multiplication factor $k=1$ (criticality): Boron steel, Cadmium, Hafnium.
     - **Coolant:** Removes fission heat to generate turbine steam: pressurized water, liquid sodium (fast reactors), $\text{CO}_2$ gas.

### Module C: Molecules & Solids

#### C1. Chemical Bonding & Interatomic Potentials
1. **Classification of Chemical Bonds:**
   - **Primary Bonds (Strong, $\sim 100 - 1000\text{ kJ/mol}$):**
     1. *Ionic:* Electrostatic attraction following complete valence electron transfer (e.g., $\text{NaCl}$). Non-directional, high melting points, brittle, insulating when solid, conducting when molten.
     2. *Covalent:* Valence electron pair sharing with directional orbital overlap (e.g., $\text{Diamond}$, $\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{Si}$). Very hard, directional, high melting point, electrical insulators/semiconductors.
     3. *Metallic:* Valence electrons delocalize into a shared "sea of electrons" surrounding a periodic lattice of positive ion cores (e.g., $\text{Al}, \text{Cu}, \text{Fe}$). Non-directional, high electrical and thermal conductivity, excellent ductility and malleability.
   - **Secondary Bonds (Weak van der Waals, $\sim 1 - 50\text{ kJ/mol}$):**
     1. *Keesom Forces:* Dipole-dipole interactions between molecules with permanent electric dipoles.
     2. *Debye Forces:* Dipole-induced dipole interactions between permanent polar molecules and polarizable neutral molecules.
     3. *London Dispersion Forces:* Fluctuating instantaneous dipole inducing temporary dipoles in adjacent atoms. Present universally in all matter; dominates noble gases and non-polar polymers.
     4. *Hydrogen Bonding:* Extra-strong dipole attraction between hydrogen bonded to tiny, highly electronegative atoms ($\text{N}, \text{O}, \text{F}$) and adjacent lone pairs.
2. **Interatomic Potential Energy Curve $U(r)$ & Bulk Material Properties:**
   - Total interatomic potential: $U(r) = U_{\text{repulsive}}(r) + U_{\text{attractive}}(r)$.
   - **Equilibrium Interatomic Separation ($r_0$):** Point of minimum potential energy where net interatomic force is zero:
     $$\vec{F} = -\nabla U \implies \left.\frac{dU}{dr}\right|_{r=r_0} = 0$$
   - **Physical Properties Deduced from $U(r)$:**
     1. **Melting / Boiling Points ($T_m, T_b$):** Governed by the depth of the potential well ($E_0 = -U(r_0)$). A deeper well indicates stronger atomic bonding, requiring greater thermal energy ($k_B T$) to break bonds $\implies$ high melting and boiling points.
     2. **Elastic Modulus (Young's Modulus $E$) & Stiffness ($k$):** Governed by the curvature of the potential well at the minimum:
        $$k = \left.\frac{d^2U}{dr^2}\right|_{r=r_0} \propto E$$
        A steeper, narrower potential well possesses a larger second derivative, translating to higher resistance to mechanical deformation (higher stiffness and Young's modulus).
     3. **Coefficient of Thermal Expansion ($\alpha$):** Governed by the **asymmetry (anharmonicity)** of the potential well. Because the repulsive wall at $r < r_0$ is steep while the attractive curve at $r > r_0$ is shallow, increasing vibrational amplitude with temperature forces the mean interatomic separation $\langle r \rangle$ to drift outwards. A deep, symmetrical well produces a low thermal expansion coefficient; a shallow, asymmetric well produces large thermal expansion.
3. **Lennard-Jones (6-12) Potential:**
   $$V(r) = 4\epsilon \left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^6\right] \quad \text{or} \quad V(r) = \frac{A}{r^{12}} - \frac{B}{r^6}$$
   - **Repulsive Term ($r^{-12}$):** Dominates at short range; arises from the Pauli exclusion principle when electron clouds overlap.
   - **Attractive Term ($r^{-6}$):** Dominates at long range; represents London dispersion forces between fluctuating dipoles.
   - **Equilibrium Separation:**
     $$\frac{dV}{dr} = 4\epsilon \left[-\frac{12\sigma^{12}}{r^{13}} + \frac{6\sigma^6}{r^7}\right] = 0 \implies r_0 = 2^{1/6}\sigma = \left(\frac{2A}{B}\right)^{1/6}$$
   - **Well Depth (Binding Energy):**
     $$E_0 = -V(r_0) = \epsilon = \frac{B^2}{4A}$$
   - **Effective Interatomic Spring Constant ($k$):**
     $$k = \left.\frac{d^2V}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0} = \frac{72\epsilon}{r_0^2} = \frac{36 B}{r_0^8}$$

#### C2. Molecular Spectroscopy (Diatomic Molecules)
1. **Rigid Rotor Rotational Energy Levels:**
   - Diatomic molecule with atomic masses $m_1, m_2$ and bond length $r_0$:
     - Reduced mass: $\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$.
     - Moment of inertia: $I = \mu r_0^2$.
   - Quantized rotational energy:
     $$E_J = \frac{\hbar^2}{2I} J(J + 1) = \frac{h^2}{8\pi^2 I} J(J + 1) = B h c J(J + 1) \quad (J = 0, 1, 2, \dots)$$
     where $B = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 I c}$ is the rotational constant.
   - Selection rule: $\Delta J = \pm 1$.
   - Transition energy: $\Delta E_{J \to J-1} = E_J - E_{J-1} = \frac{\hbar^2}{I} J = 2 B h c J$.
   - Longest transition wavelength occurs for $J = 1 \to 0$:
     $$\Delta E_{1\to 0} = \frac{\hbar^2}{I} = \frac{h^2}{4\pi^2 I} \implies \lambda_{\max} = \frac{h c}{\Delta E} = \frac{4\pi^2 c I}{h}$$
   - **Isotope Effect & Doublet Splitting:** Substituting a heavier isotope (e.g., $^{37}\text{Cl}$ for $^{35}\text{Cl}$) leaves equilibrium bond length $r_0$ unchanged but increases reduced mass $\mu$, raising moment of inertia $I$. Consequently, rotational energy levels compress, transition energies decrease, and emitted/absorbed wavelengths shift to longer values (redshift):
     $$\mu \uparrow \implies I \uparrow \implies \Delta E \downarrow \implies \lambda \uparrow$$
2. **Harmonic Oscillator Vibrational Energy Levels:**
   - Potential modeled as parabolic well $U(x) = \frac{1}{2} k x^2$:
     $$E_v = \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar \omega_0 = \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right) h f_0 \quad (v = 0, 1, 2, \dots)$$
     where fundamental vibrational frequency is $\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}} \implies f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}}$.
   - **Zero-Point Energy ($v = 0$):** $E_0 = \frac{1}{2}\hbar \omega_0 = \frac{1}{2} h f_0 > 0$. The molecule vibrates even at absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$), an inescapable quantum result of Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.
   - Level spacing between consecutive states is constant:
     $$\Delta E = E_{v+1} - E_v = \hbar \omega_0 = h f_0 = \frac{h}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}}$$
   - Classical amplitude of vibration in state $v$:
     $$E_v = \frac{1}{2} k A_v^2 \implies A_v = \sqrt{\frac{2 E_v}{k}} = \sqrt{\frac{(2v + 1)\hbar \omega_0}{k}}$$
   - Molecular absorption comparison: Higher bond spring constant $k$ (stronger bond, e.g. $\text{C}=\text{C}$ vs $\text{C}-\text{C}$ or $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$ vs $\text{I}_2$) and lower reduced mass $\mu$ yield a higher vibrational frequency $\omega_0$, absorbing higher-energy photons.
3. **Band Theory of Solids:**
   - **Conductors:** Valence band is partially filled, or completely filled valence band overlaps with empty conduction band ($E_g = 0$). High electron mobility.
   - **Semiconductors:** Completely filled valence band separated from empty conduction band by a narrow band gap ($E_g \sim 1\text{ eV}$, e.g. $\text{Si} = 1.1\text{ eV}, \text{Ge} = 0.67\text{ eV}$). Thermal excitation across gap enables conductivity at room temperature.
   - **Insulators:** Large forbidden energy band gap ($E_g > 5\text{ eV}$, e.g. diamond $= 5.5\text{ eV}$). Negligible thermal excitation; electrons remain localized in valence bonds.

#### Problem 1: Electric Dipole in an External Uniform Electric Field
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 01; PH 102 2014/2015)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) An electric dipole consists of two point charges of equal magnitude $q$ and opposite sign separated by a distance $2a$.  
(i) Define the electric dipole moment of a dipole.  
(ii) Write down an expression for the dipole moment vector $\vec{p}$, defining all symbols and stating its direction.  
(iii) The dipole is placed in a uniform external electric field $\vec{E}$ at an angle $\theta$ with respect to the field lines.  
1. Derive an expression for the torque $\vec{\tau}$ acting on the dipole.  
2. Show that $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$.  
(b) The potential energy of the dipole in the field is given by $U = -(\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E})$. Consider an electric dipole consisting of charges $\pm 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$ separated by $0.125\text{ nm}$, placed in a uniform electric field of $5.00 \times 10^5\text{ N/C}$ at an angle of $35.0^\circ$ to the field.  
(i) Determine the net force exerted by the field on the dipole.  
(ii) Find the magnitude and direction of the electric dipole moment.  
(iii) Determine the magnitude and direction of the torque acting on the dipole.  
(iv) Estimate the potential energy of the dipole in the position shown.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a)(i) Definition:**  
The **electric dipole moment** is a vector measure of the separation of positive and negative electrical charges within a system, defined as the product of the magnitude of one of the charges and the displacement vector between them.

**(a)(ii) Vector Expression & Direction:**  
$$\vec{p} = q (2\vec{a})$$
where $q$ is the magnitude of either point charge, and $2\vec{a}$ is the displacement vector directed from the negative charge ($-q$) to the positive charge ($+q$). The direction of $\vec{p}$ is along the dipole axis pointing **from negative to positive**.

**(a)(iii) Derivation of Torque:**  
1. In a uniform electric field $\vec{E}$, the force on $+q$ is $\vec{F}_+ = +q\vec{E}$, and the force on $-q$ is $\vec{F}_- = -q\vec{E}$.  
The net force is $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{F}_+ + \vec{F}_- = \vec{0}$.  
Because the two equal and opposite forces act along different lines of action, they form a couple that produces a net torque about the dipole center $O$:
$$\tau = F_+ (a\sin\theta) + F_- (a\sin\theta) = (qE)(a\sin\theta) + (qE)(a\sin\theta) = 2qa E \sin\theta$$
2. Since $p = 2qa$, the magnitude of the torque is $\tau = p E \sin\theta$.  
By the right-hand rule, the torque tends to rotate $\vec{p}$ into alignment with $\vec{E}$ (clockwise in standard orientation), perpendicular to both $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{E}$. Therefore, in vector form:
$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E} \quad \text{[Q.E.D.]}$$

**(b)(i) Net Force:**  
$$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{F}_+ + \vec{F}_- = (+q\vec{E}) + (-q\vec{E}) = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}$$

**(b)(ii) Dipole Moment Magnitude & Direction:**  
$$p = q(2a) = (1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) \times (0.125 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}) = \mathbf{2.00 \times 10^{-29}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}}$$  
*Direction:* Along the dipole axis, directed from $-1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$ to $+1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$.

**(b)(iii) Torque Magnitude & Direction:**  
$$\tau = p E \sin\theta = (2.00 \times 10^{-29}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}) \times (5.00 \times 10^5\text{ N/C}) \times \sin(35.0^\circ)$$
$$\tau = (1.00 \times 10^{-23}) \times 0.57358 = \mathbf{5.74 \times 10^{-24}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}$$  
*Direction:* Perpendicular to the plane formed by $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{E}$, acting in the direction that rotates the dipole toward alignment with $\vec{E}$ (clockwise / into the page).

**(b)(iv) Potential Energy:**  
$$U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -p E \cos\theta = -(2.00 \times 10^{-29}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}) \times (5.00 \times 10^5\text{ N/C}) \times \cos(35.0^\circ)$$
$$U = -(1.00 \times 10^{-23}) \times 0.81915 = \mathbf{-8.19 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J}}$$

#### Problem 2: Gauss's Law, Cylindrical Conductor & Orbiting Electron Dynamics
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 02; Lecture Notes & Handwritten e.g. 4)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) (i) State Gauss's Theorem in electrostatics.  
(ii) A long straight cylindrical conductor of radius $a$ carries a positive linear charge density $\lambda$ ($\text{C/m}$).  
1. Define linear charge density.  
2. Derive an expression for the electric field intensity $E(r)$ at a radial distance $r \ge a$ from the central axis.  
3. Sketch a graph showing the variation of $E(r)$ with $r$ from $r=0$ to $r \gg a$ for: (I) a conducting cylinder, and (II) a solid non-conducting cylinder with uniform volume charge density $\rho$.  
(b) An electron (charge $-e$, mass $m_e$) orbits the positively charged cylindrical conductor in a stable circular path of radius $r$ ($r > a$) centered on the axis. Neglecting gravitational effects:  
(i) Indicate the force acting on the electron and write down its magnitude in terms of $\lambda, r, \epsilon_0, e$.  
(ii) Obtain an expression for the angular velocity $\omega$ of the electron.  
(iii) Obtain an expression for the tangential speed $v$ of the electron.  
(iv) Calculate the orbital period $T$ of the electron given $\lambda = 6.00 \times 10^{-15}\text{ C/m}$ and $r = 5.00\ \mu\text{m}$.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a)(i) Gauss's Theorem:**  
The total electric flux $\Phi_E$ passing through any closed imaginary surface (Gaussian surface) is equal to $\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}$ times the net electric charge $Q_{\text{enc}}$ enclosed by that surface:
$$\oint_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0}$$

**(a)(ii)(1) Linear Charge Density:**  
The charge per unit length along a line or one-dimensional conductor: $\lambda = \frac{dq}{dl}$, measured in $\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}$.

**(a)(ii)(2) Derivation of $E(r)$ for $r \ge a$:**  
1. Construct a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ ($r \ge a$) and length $l$.  
2. By cylindrical symmetry, $\vec{E}$ is directed radially outward everywhere.  
3. The surface consists of three sections: two flat circular end caps ($S_1, S_3$) and the curved cylindrical body ($S_2$):
   - Flat end caps: $\vec{E} \perp d\vec{A} \implies \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = 0$.
   - Curved body: $\vec{E} \parallel d\vec{A}$ and magnitude $E$ is uniform over radius $r$:
$$\Phi_E = \int_{S_1} 0 + \int_{S_3} 0 + \int_{S_2} E \, dA = E(2\pi r l)$$
4. The enclosed charge is $Q_{\text{enc}} = \lambda l$.  
5. Applying Gauss's Theorem:
$$E (2\pi r l) = \frac{\lambda l}{\epsilon_0} \implies E(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r} \quad (r \ge a)$$

**(a)(ii)(3) Graphs of $E(r)$ vs $r$:**  
- **Case I: Conducting Cylinder (radius $a$):**
  - For $r < a$: $E = 0$ (all electrostatic charge resides on the outer surface).
  - At $r = a$: $E$ jumps discontinuously from $0$ to $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 a} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}$.
  - For $r \ge a$: $E(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r} \propto \frac{1}{r}$ (hyperbolic decay).
- **Case II: Solid Non-Conducting Cylinder with Uniform $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\pi a^2}$:**
  - For $r \le a$: Gaussian cylinder of radius $r$ encloses $Q_{\text{enc}} = \rho(\pi r^2 l) = \lambda l \frac{r^2}{a^2}$.
    $$E(2\pi r l) = \frac{\lambda l r^2}{\epsilon_0 a^2} \implies E(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 a^2} r \propto r \quad (\text{linear increase from } 0 \text{ to } r=a)$$
  - For $r \ge a$: $E(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r} \propto \frac{1}{r}$.

**(b)(i) Force Acting on Electron:**  
The force is purely attractive and directed radially inward toward the cylinder axis:
$$F = e E(r) = \frac{e \lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r}$$

**(b)(ii) Angular Velocity $\omega$:**  
Centripetal force is provided by the electrostatic force:
$$F_c = m_e \omega^2 r = \frac{e \lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r} \implies \omega^2 = \frac{e \lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 m_e r^2} \implies \omega = \frac{1}{r} \sqrt{\frac{e \lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 m_e}}$$

**(b)(iii) Tangential Speed $v$:**  
$$v = \omega r = \sqrt{\frac{e \lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 m_e}}$$
*(Crucial Remark: The orbital speed $v$ is completely independent of the orbit radius $r$!)*

**(b)(iv) Evaluation of Orbital Period $T$:**  
$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = 2\pi r \sqrt{\frac{2\pi \epsilon_0 m_e}{e \lambda}}$$
Let us evaluate numerically:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{(1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) \times (6.00 \times 10^{-15}\text{ C/m})}{2\pi \times (8.85 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F/m}) \times (9.11 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg})}}$$
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{9.60 \times 10^{-34}}{5.066 \times 10^{-41}}} = \sqrt{1.895 \times 10^7} \approx 4.353 \times 10^3\text{ m/s}$$
Now computing $T$ for $r = 5.00 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$:
$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi \times 5.00 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}}{4.353 \times 10^3\text{ m/s}} = \frac{3.1416 \times 10^{-5}}{4353} \approx \mathbf{7.22 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s}} = \mathbf{7.22\text{ ns}}$$

#### Problem 3: Parallel Plate Capacitor with Partial Dielectric Slab
*(Source: Official Coursework In-Class Problem 2; Practice Sheet Questions 7 & 8)*

**Problem Statement:**  
A parallel-plate capacitor has plate area $A = 115\text{ cm}^2$ and plate separation $d = 1.24\text{ cm}$. A potential difference $V_0 = 85.5\text{ V}$ is applied across the plates using a battery. The battery is then **disconnected**. A dielectric slab of thickness $b = 0.780\text{ cm}$ and dielectric constant $\kappa = 2.61$ is inserted between the plates.  
(a) What is the capacitance $C_0$ before the dielectric slab is inserted?  
(b) What free charge $q$ appears on the capacitor plates?  
(c) What is the electric field $E_0$ in the remaining air gaps between the plates and the slab?  
(d) What is the electric field $E_1$ inside the dielectric slab?  
(e) What is the potential difference $V$ between the plates after the slab is introduced?  
(f) What is the capacitance $C$ with the dielectric slab in place?

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**Conversion of Units to SI:**  
- $A = 115\text{ cm}^2 = 115 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = 0.0115\text{ m}^2$  
- $d = 1.24\text{ cm} = 1.24 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m} = 0.0124\text{ m}$  
- $b = 0.780\text{ cm} = 0.780 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m} = 0.00780\text{ m}$  
- Air gap thickness: $d - b = 0.0124 - 0.00780 = 0.00460\text{ m} = 0.460\text{ cm}$

**(a) Initial Capacitance $C_0$:**  
$$C_0 = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{(8.85 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F/m}) \times (0.0115\text{ m}^2)}{0.0124\text{ m}} = \mathbf{8.21 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F}} = \mathbf{8.21\text{ pF}}$$

**(b) Free Charge $q$ on Plates:**  
Because the battery is disconnected after charging, this free charge is trapped and conserved:
$$q = C_0 V_0 = (8.2105 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F}) \times (85.5\text{ V}) = \mathbf{7.02 \times 10^{-10}\text{ C}} = \mathbf{0.702\text{ nC}}$$

**(c) Electric Field in Air Gaps $E_0$:**  
Since surface charge density $\sigma = \frac{q}{A}$ is unchanged:
$$E_0 = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0 A} = \frac{V_0}{d} = \frac{85.5\text{ V}}{0.0124\text{ m}} = \mathbf{6.90 \times 10^3\text{ V/m}} = \mathbf{6.90\text{ kV/m}}$$

**(d) Electric Field inside Dielectric Slab $E_1$:**  
Dielectric polarization reduces the electric field by a factor of $\kappa$:
$$E_1 = \frac{E_0}{\kappa} = \frac{6895.16\text{ V/m}}{2.61} = \mathbf{2.64 \times 10^3\text{ V/m}} = \mathbf{2.64\text{ kV/m}}$$

**(e) Potential Difference $V$ across Plates:**  
Integrating the electric field along the path between the two plates:
$$V = \int E \, dx = E_0(d - b) + E_1 b$$
$$V = (6895.16\text{ V/m}) \times (0.00460\text{ m}) + (2641.82\text{ V/m}) \times (0.00780\text{ m})$$
$$V = 31.718\text{ V} + 20.606\text{ V} = \mathbf{52.32\text{ V}}$$

**(f) Capacitance $C$ with Slab Inserted:**  
$$C = \frac{q}{V} = \frac{7.020 \times 10^{-10}\text{ C}}{52.324\text{ V}} = \mathbf{1.34 \times 10^{-11}\text{ F}} = \mathbf{13.4\text{ pF}}$$
*(Alternative verification via series combination: $\frac{1}{C} = \frac{d-b}{\epsilon_0 A} + \frac{b}{\kappa \epsilon_0 A} \implies C = 13.41\text{ pF}$. Perfect consistency!)*

#### Problem 4: Ampère's Circuital Law & Thick Wire with Non-Uniform Current Density
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 03; 2022/2023 Question 02; Handwritten Magnetostatics Past Papers)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) State Ampère's Circuital Law and write it in integral form, clearly defining all symbols used.  
(b) An infinitely long, straight cylindrical conductor of radius $a$ carries a steady total current $I$ along its axis. The current density varies with radial distance $r$ from the axis according to:
$$J(r) = J_0\left(1 - \frac{r}{a}\right) \quad (0 \le r \le a)$$
where $J_0$ is the peak current density at the central axis.  
(i) Find an expression for the total current $I$ in terms of $J_0$ and $a$.  
(ii) Using Ampère's law, derive expressions for the magnetic field intensity $B(r)$:  
1. Inside the conductor ($r < a$).  
2. Outside the conductor ($r \ge a$).  
(iii) Show that outside the conductor, $B(r)$ is identical to that of a thin wire carrying current $I$.  
(iv) Plot the variation of $B(r)$ with $r$, and determine the value of $r$ where $B(r)$ attains its maximum.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Ampère's Circuital Law:**  
The line integral of magnetic field intensity $\vec{B}$ around any closed closed loop (Ampèrian loop) is equal to $\mu_0$ times the total steady electric current $I_{\text{enc}}$ threading through the loop:
$$\oint_{\mathcal{C}} \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{\ell} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$$
where $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic flux density vector, $d\vec{\ell}$ is the infinitesimal vector path element along the closed loop $\mathcal{C}$, $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space ($4\pi \times 10^{-7}\text{ H/m}$), and $I_{\text{enc}}$ is the net electric current enclosed by $\mathcal{C}$.

**(b)(i) Total Current $I$:**  
Divide the circular cross-section into concentric annular rings of radius $r'$ and thickness $dr'$. Area of each ring is $dA = 2\pi r' dr'$.
$$I = \int_0^a J(r') dA = \int_0^a J_0\left(1 - \frac{r'}{a}\right) (2\pi r' dr')$$
$$I = 2\pi J_0 \int_0^a \left(r' - \frac{r'^2}{a}\right) dr' = 2\pi J_0 \left[ \frac{r'^2}{2} - \frac{r'^3}{3a} \right]_0^a = 2\pi J_0 \left( \frac{a^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{3} \right)$$
$$I = 2\pi J_0 \left(\frac{a^2}{6}\right) = \frac{\pi J_0 a^2}{3} \implies J_0 = \frac{3I}{\pi a^2}$$

**(b)(ii)(1) Magnetic Field Inside ($r < a$):**  
Choose a circular Ampèrian loop of radius $r$ ($r < a$) concentric with the cylinder. By axial symmetry, $\vec{B}$ is tangential to the loop and has constant magnitude $B(r)$:
$$\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{\ell} = B(r) \oint dl = B(r) (2\pi r)$$
The enclosed current $I_{\text{enc}}(r)$ is:
$$I_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r J(r') (2\pi r' dr') = 2\pi J_0 \left[ \frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^3}{3a} \right] = \pi J_0 r^2 \left( 1 - \frac{2r}{3a} \right)$$
Applying Ampère's law:
$$B(r) \cdot (2\pi r) = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}(r) = \mu_0 \pi J_0 r^2 \left( 1 - \frac{2r}{3a} \right)$$
$$B(r) = \frac{\mu_0 J_0 r}{2} \left( 1 - \frac{2r}{3a} \right)$$
Substituting $J_0 = \frac{3I}{\pi a^2}$:
$$B(r) = \frac{\mu_0}{2} \left(\frac{3I}{\pi a^2}\right) r \left( 1 - \frac{2r}{3a} \right) = \frac{3\mu_0 I}{2\pi a^2} r \left( 1 - \frac{2r}{3a} \right) \quad (r < a)$$

**(b)(ii)(2) Magnetic Field Outside ($r \ge a$):**  
For an Ampèrian loop of radius $r \ge a$, the entire current $I$ is enclosed: $I_{\text{enc}} = I$.
$$B(r) \cdot (2\pi r) = \mu_0 I \implies B(r) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} \quad (r \ge a)$$

**(b)(iii) Thin Wire Reduction:**  
For any point outside ($r \ge a$), $B(r) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, which depends only on the total current $I$ and radial distance $r$. It is completely independent of the conductor radius $a$ or the internal current distribution $J(r)$, matching the field of a line current carrying $I$ along the axis.

**(b)(iv) Peak Field Location:**  
To find the maximum inside the wire, set $\frac{dB}{dr} = 0$:
$$\frac{d}{dr}\left(r - \frac{2r^2}{3a}\right) = 1 - \frac{4r}{3a} = 0 \implies r_{\max} = \frac{3}{4}a = 0.75 a$$
At $r = \frac{3}{4}a$:
$$B_{\max} = \frac{3\mu_0 I}{2\pi a^2}\left(\frac{3}{4}a\right)\left(1 - \frac{2}{3a}\frac{3a}{4}\right) = \frac{9\mu_0 I}{8\pi a}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{9\mu_0 I}{16\pi a} \approx 0.5625 \frac{\mu_0 I}{\pi a}$$
Whereas at the surface $r=a$:
$$B(a) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a} = 0.500 \frac{\mu_0 I}{\pi a} < B_{\max}$$
*Graph Characteristics:* $B(r)$ starts from $0$ at $r=0$, rises along a quadratic curve to a maximum at $r = \frac{3}{4}a$, decreases to $B(a)$ at the surface, and thereafter decays hyperbolically as $1/r$.

#### Problem 5: Lorentz Force, Zero Work Proof & Finite Field Deflection
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2022/2023 Question 03; Handwritten Magnetostatics Past Papers p4–5)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) (i) What is the Lorentz force? Explain the factors that determine its magnitude and direction.  
(ii) Prove that the magnetic component of the Lorentz force does **no work** on a moving charged particle.  
(b) A charged particle of mass $m$ and positive charge $q$ travels at speed $v$ along the $+x$-axis and enters a uniform magnetic field $\vec{B}$ directed into the page. The field is confined to a slab of width $d$.  
(i) Derive an expression for the radius of curvature $R$ of the particle's path.  
(ii) If the width of the magnetic field region is $d$, find the deflection angle $\theta$ and the time $t$ spent inside the magnetic field for:  
1. $d = \frac{mv}{2qB}$  
2. $d = \frac{mv}{qB}$  
3. $d = \frac{2mv}{qB}$

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a)(i) Lorentz Force:**  
The total electromagnetic force experienced by a particle carrying charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec{v}$ in electric and magnetic fields:
$$\vec{F} = q\vec{E} + q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$$
The magnetic component is $\vec{F}_B = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$.
- Magnitude: $F_B = |q| v B \sin\phi$, where $\phi$ is the angle between velocity $\vec{v}$ and magnetic field $\vec{B}$.
- Direction: Determined by the vector cross product (right-hand rule or Fleming's left-hand rule), acting strictly perpendicular to both $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$.

**(a)(ii) Proof that Magnetic Force Does Zero Work:**  
The work done by a force along an infinitesimal displacement $d\vec{s} = \vec{v} dt$ is:
$$dW = \vec{F}_B \cdot d\vec{s} = \vec{F}_B \cdot (\vec{v} dt) = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \cdot \vec{v} \, dt$$
By the properties of the vector scalar triple product, the cross product $(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$ is orthogonal to $\vec{v}$:
$$(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \cdot \vec{v} \equiv 0 \implies dW = 0$$
Hence, the total work done $W = \int dW = 0$. By the Work-Energy Theorem ($\Delta K = W = 0$), the particle's speed and kinetic energy remain strictly constant. The magnetic force acts solely as a deflecting (centripetal) force.

**(b)(i) Radius of Curvature $R$:**  
Inside the field, $\vec{v} \perp \vec{B} \implies \sin\phi = 1$. The magnetic force provides the required centripetal acceleration:
$$F_B = q v B = \frac{m v^2}{R} \implies R = \frac{m v}{q B}$$

**(b)(ii) Deflection Angle $\theta$ and Transit Time $t$:**  
From the trajectory geometry (arc of circle of radius $R$ intersecting boundary $x = d$):
$$\sin\theta = \frac{d}{R} = \frac{q B d}{m v}$$
The arc length traversed is $s = R \theta$. Since speed $v$ is constant, the time spent is:
$$t = \frac{s}{v} = \frac{R \theta}{v} = \frac{\theta}{\omega} = \theta \left(\frac{m}{q B}\right)$$
The full orbital period is $T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}$.

1. **For $d = \frac{mv}{2qB} = \frac{R}{2}$:**
   $$\sin\theta = \frac{R/2}{R} = \frac{1}{2} \implies \mathbf{\theta = 30.0^\circ} = \frac{\pi}{6}\text{ rad}$$
   $$t = \frac{\pi/6}{2\pi} T = \frac{T}{12} = \mathbf{\frac{\pi m}{6 q B}}$$

2. **For $d = \frac{mv}{qB} = R$:**
   $$\sin\theta = \frac{R}{R} = 1 \implies \mathbf{\theta = 90.0^\circ} = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{ rad}$$
   $$t = \frac{\pi/2}{2\pi} T = \frac{T}{4} = \mathbf{\frac{\pi m}{2 q B}}$$

3. **For $d = \frac{2mv}{qB} = 2R$:**  
   Because $d > R$, the particle cannot traverse the full width $d$. Instead, the magnetic force bends the particle through a full semicircle of diameter $2R$, exiting the field back across the original entrance boundary ($x=0$):
   $$\mathbf{\theta = 180^\circ} = \pi\text{ rad}$$
   $$t = \frac{\pi}{2\pi} T = \frac{T}{2} = \mathbf{\frac{\pi m}{q B}}$$

#### Problem 6: Blackbody Radiation, Stefan-Boltzmann & Wien's Displacement Laws
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 04; Modern Physics Tutorial 01)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) Using the Stefan-Boltzmann law of blackbody radiation, write down an expression for the total emissive power $P$ of a blackbody at absolute temperature $T$.  
(b) The total emissive power per unit area of a blackbody is measured to be $5.67 \times 10^8\text{ W/m}^2$. Find the surface temperature of the body.  
(c) State Wien's displacement law as an equation and identify each symbol.  
(d) Sketch blackbody radiation curves (spectral radiance $I_\lambda$ vs $\lambda$) for four different temperatures ($T_4 > T_3 > T_2 > T_1$), highlighting how the peak shifts.  
(e) The surface temperature of a hot star is $10,000\text{ K}$. Assuming it radiates as an ideal blackbody ($\epsilon = 1$):  
(i) Calculate the wavelength $\lambda_{\max}$ corresponding to peak radiation intensity.  
(ii) Identify the region of the electromagnetic spectrum to which this peak belongs.  
(iii) Determine the energy (in $\text{eV}$) of a photon corresponding to this peak wavelength.  
(iv) If the star has radius $R = 8.50 \times 10^8\text{ m}$, calculate the total rate of energy emission (luminosity) and estimate the rate at which photons leave its surface.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Stefan-Boltzmann Law:**  
$$P = \sigma A T^4 \quad \text{or for total power per unit area (intensity): } I = \frac{P}{A} = \sigma T^4$$
where $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\text{ W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4}$ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, $A$ is surface area, and $T$ is absolute temperature in Kelvin.

**(b) Temperature Calculation:**  
$$I = \sigma T^4 \implies T = \left(\frac{I}{\sigma}\right)^{1/4}$$
$$T = \left(\frac{5.67 \times 10^8\text{ W/m}^2}{5.67 \times 10^{-8}\text{ W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4}}\right)^{1/4} = (10^{16}\text{ K}^4)^{1/4} = \mathbf{10,000\text{ K}} = \mathbf{1.00 \times 10^4\text{ K}}$$

**(c) Wien's Displacement Law:**  
$$\lambda_m T = C_W$$
where $\lambda_m$ is the wavelength at which the spectral radiance is a maximum (peak wavelength), $T$ is the absolute temperature of the blackbody in Kelvin, and $C_W = 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}\cdot\text{K}$ (commonly given as $2.90 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}\cdot\text{K}$) is Wien's displacement constant.

**(d) Spectral Radiance Curves ($I_\lambda$ vs $\lambda$):**  
- As $T$ increases ($T_4 > T_3 > T_2 > T_1$):
  1. The overall curve rises at every single wavelength; the total area underneath the curve (total power per unit area) scales as $T^4$.
  2. The peak of the curve ($\lambda_m$) shifts systematically toward shorter wavelengths (higher frequency / higher photon energy), following the dashed hyperbola $\lambda_m T = \text{constant}$.

**(e)(i) Peak Wavelength $\lambda_{\max}$:**  
$$\lambda_{\max} = \frac{C_W}{T} = \frac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}\cdot\text{K}}{10,000\text{ K}} = \mathbf{2.898 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{289.8\text{ nm}} \approx \mathbf{290\text{ nm}}$$

**(e)(ii) Electromagnetic Spectrum Region:**  
Since $290\text{ nm}$ is below the visible spectrum ($400 - 700\text{ nm}$), it lies in the **Ultraviolet (UV)** region (specifically UV-B / UV-C).

**(e)(iii) Photon Energy in eV:**  
$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda_{\max}} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}) \times (3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s})}{2.898 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}} = 6.863 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$$
Converting to electron-volts:
$$E = \frac{6.863 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J/eV}} = \mathbf{4.29\text{ eV}}$$

**(e)(iv) Luminosity & Photon Emission Rate:**  
Surface area of spherical star:
$$A = 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi (8.50 \times 10^8\text{ m})^2 \approx 9.079 \times 10^{18}\text{ m}^2$$
Total rate of energy emission (Power):
$$P = \sigma A T^4 = (5.67 \times 10^{-8}\text{ W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4}) \times (9.079 \times 10^{18}\text{ m}^2) \times (10,000\text{ K})^4$$
$$P = (5.148 \times 10^{11}) \times 10^{16} = \mathbf{5.15 \times 10^{27}\text{ W}}$$
Rate of photon emission ($N_{\text{photons}}/s$):
$$\frac{dN_p}{dt} \approx \frac{P}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{5.148 \times 10^{27}\text{ J/s}}{6.863 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J/photon}} \approx \mathbf{7.50 \times 10^{45}\text{ photons/second}}$$

#### Problem 7: Photoelectric Effect, Work Function & Photocell Current
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 05; Tutorial Sheets 01 & 02)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) Write down Einstein's photoelectric equation and identify each symbol.  
(b) Monochromatic light of wavelength $400\text{ nm}$ falls on a metallic surface whose work function is $2.00\text{ eV}$.  
(i) Calculate the threshold wavelength $\lambda_0$ of the metal.  
(ii) Calculate the maximum kinetic energy $K_{\max}$ of the emitted photoelectrons in $\text{eV}$ and Joules.  
(iii) What is the stopping potential $V_s$ required to reduce the photocurrent to zero?  
(c) A narrow, parallel beam of light of wavelength $546\text{ nm}$ from an $80.0\text{ mW}$ source falls on the cathode of a photocell.  
(i) How many photons strike the cathode per second?  
(ii) If only $1.50\%$ of incident photons succeed in liberating electrons (quantum efficiency $\eta = 0.015$), calculate the resulting saturation photocell current.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Einstein's Photoelectric Equation:**  
$$K_{\max} = hf - \Phi = \frac{hc}{\lambda} - \Phi = e V_s$$
where:
- $K_{\max}$: Maximum kinetic energy of the ejected photoelectrons.
- $h$: Planck's constant ($6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$).
- $f$: Frequency of the incident electromagnetic radiation ($c/\lambda$).
- $\Phi$: Work function of the target metal (minimum energy to extract an electron).
- $e$: Elementary electronic charge ($1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$).
- $V_s$: Stopping potential (potential difference required to halt the fastest photoelectrons).

**(b)(i) Threshold Wavelength $\lambda_0$:**  
$$\Phi = h f_0 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_0} \implies \lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{\Phi}$$
Converting $\Phi = 2.00\text{ eV} = 2.00 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 3.20 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$:
$$\lambda_0 = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}) \times (3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s})}{3.20 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}} = \frac{1.989 \times 10^{-25}}{3.20 \times 10^{-19}} = \mathbf{6.22 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{622\text{ nm}}$$

**(b)(ii) Maximum Kinetic Energy $K_{\max}$:**  
Incident photon energy:
$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{1.989 \times 10^{-25}\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}}{400 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}} = 4.9725 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$$
In electron-volts:
$$E = \frac{4.9725 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J/eV}} \approx 3.108\text{ eV}$$
Maximum kinetic energy:
$$K_{\max} = E - \Phi = 3.108\text{ eV} - 2.00\text{ eV} = \mathbf{1.11\text{ eV}}$$
In Joules:
$$K_{\max} = 1.108 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = \mathbf{1.77 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}}$$

**(b)(iii) Stopping Potential $V_s$:**  
Since $e V_s = K_{\max}$:
$$V_s = \frac{K_{\max}}{e} = \frac{1.108\text{ eV}}{e} = \mathbf{1.11\text{ V}}$$

#### Problem 8: Compton Scattering Derivation, Assumptions & Energy Shift
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2022/2023 Question 04; Tutorial 02 Question 12)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) What is the Compton Effect, and why did it provide crucial historical evidence for the quantum theory of light?  
(b) Write down the Compton wavelength shift formula, identifying all symbols and detailing the three fundamental physical assumptions on which the derivation rests.  
(c) Explain why an unmodified line ($\Delta \lambda = 0$) is always observed alongside the scattered shifted line in experimental Compton spectra.  
(d) X-rays of wavelength $\lambda = 0.200\text{ nm}$ are scattered from a graphite block. The scattered X-rays are observed at an angle of $\theta = 45.0^\circ$ relative to the incident beam.  
(i) Calculate the wavelength $\lambda'$ of the scattered X-rays at this angle.  
(ii) Compute the fractional energy lost by the photon during this collision.  
(iii) What is the theoretical maximum wavelength shift possible in this scattering experiment, and at what angle does it occur?

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Definition & Physical Significance:**  
The **Compton Effect** is the increase in wavelength (decrease in photon energy and frequency) observed when high-frequency electromagnetic radiation (X-rays or $\gamma$-rays) is scattered elastically by loosely bound electrons in matter. Classical electromagnetic wave theory predicted that scattered light must have the exact same frequency as the incident wave. Compton scattering proved that photons possess discrete, directed particle-like momentum ($p = h/\lambda$) and undergo billiard-ball-like relativistic elastic collisions.

**(b) Formula, Symbols & Assumptions:**  
$$\Delta \lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_e c}(1 - \cos\theta) = \lambda_c (1 - \cos\theta)$$
- Symbols:
  - $\lambda$: Incident photon wavelength.
  - $\lambda'$: Scattered photon wavelength.
  - $\Delta \lambda$: Compton wavelength shift.
  - $\theta$: Photon scattering angle relative to the forward direction.
  - $h$: Planck's constant ($6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$).
  - $m_e$: Electron rest mass ($9.11 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}$).
  - $c$: Speed of light ($3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$).
  - $\lambda_c = \frac{h}{m_e c} \approx 2.426 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m} = 2.426\text{ pm}$ (Compton wavelength of electron).
- Core Assumptions:
  1. Radiation consists of localized, particle-like photons possessing energy $E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$ and momentum $p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{E}{c}$.
  2. The target electron is initially stationary and quasi-free (its atomic binding energy is negligible compared to the incident photon energy: $E_{\text{binding}} \ll E_{\text{photon}}$).
  3. The interaction is an isolated, relativistic elastic collision in two dimensions where relativistic total energy and relativistic momentum are strictly conserved:
     $$\vec{p}_\gamma = \vec{p}'_\gamma + \vec{p}_e \quad \text{and} \quad E_\gamma + m_e c^2 = E'_\gamma + \sqrt{p_e^2 c^2 + m_e^2 c^4}$$

**(c) Origin of the Unmodified Line ($\Delta \lambda = 0$):**  
In any material, some photons strike tightly bound inner-shell electrons (e.g. $K$-shell). The binding energy of these electrons is comparable to or exceeds the photon energy; they cannot be knocked free alone. Consequently, the photon scatters elastically off the **entire atom** of mass $M_{\text{atom}}$. In the shift formula, replacing $m_e$ with $M_{\text{atom}}$ gives:
$$\Delta \lambda_{\text{atom}} = \frac{h}{M_{\text{atom}} c}(1 - \cos\theta)$$
Since $M_{\text{atom}} \approx 10^4 - 10^5 m_e$, $\Delta \lambda_{\text{atom}} \approx 0$. These photons emerge with undetectable wavelength shift, forming the central unmodified Thomson line.

**(d)(i) Scattered Wavelength Calculation:**  
For $\lambda = 0.200\text{ nm} = 2.00 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$ and $\theta = 45.0^\circ$:
$$\Delta \lambda = \lambda_c (1 - \cos 45.0^\circ) = (2.426 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m}) \times (1 - 0.70711)$$
$$\Delta \lambda = (2.426 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m}) \times 0.29289 \approx 7.106 \times 10^{-13}\text{ m} = 0.000711\text{ nm}$$
$$\lambda' = \lambda + \Delta \lambda = 0.200\text{ nm} + 0.000711\text{ nm} = \mathbf{0.200711\text{ nm}} = \mathbf{2.0071 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}}$$

**(d)(ii) Fractional Energy Change:**  
$$\frac{|\Delta E|}{E} = \frac{E - E'}{E} = \frac{\frac{hc}{\lambda} - \frac{hc}{\lambda'}}{\frac{hc}{\lambda}} = \frac{\lambda' - \lambda}{\lambda'} = \frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda'}$$
$$\frac{|\Delta E|}{E} = \frac{0.0007106\text{ nm}}{0.200711\text{ nm}} \approx \mathbf{3.54 \times 10^{-3}} = \mathbf{0.354\%}$$

**(d)(iii) Maximum Wavelength Shift:**  
The shift is maximized when $(1 - \cos\theta)$ is maximized, which occurs at backscattering $\theta = 180^\circ$ ($\cos 180^\circ = -1$):
$$\Delta \lambda_{\max} = \lambda_c [1 - (-1)] = 2\lambda_c = 2 \times 2.426 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m} = \mathbf{4.85 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{4.85\text{ pm}}$$

#### Problem 9: Bohr Model Energy Derivation & Pickering Series in $\text{He}^+$
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2022/2023 Question 06; 2013/2014 Question 05; Lecture 06)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) State Bohr's three fundamental postulates for the hydrogen-like atom.  
(b) (i) Derive an expression for the quantized energy levels $E_n$ of an electron orbiting a hydrogen-like nucleus with atomic number $Z$.  
(ii) Hence, derive the general formula for the wavelength $\lambda$ of the photon emitted during an electronic transition from initial level $n_i$ to final level $n_f$.  
(c) In 1896, the American astronomer Edward Pickering observed a series of absorption/emission lines in the spectrum of the star $\zeta$-Puppis that obeyed the empirical formula:
$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{(m/2)^2}\right) \quad (m = 5, 6, 7, \dots)$$
Show that these lines are completely explained by Bohr's theory as originating from singly ionized Helium ($\text{He}^+, Z=2$), and explain why some of these lines coincided with the Balmer series of Hydrogen while others appeared between them.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Bohr's Postulates:**  
1. **Stationary Orbits:** Electrons revolve in non-radiating, stable circular orbits around the central nucleus governed by electrostatic Coulomb attraction.  
2. **Quantization of Angular Momentum:** Only those orbits are permitted in which orbital angular momentum $L$ is an integer multiple of $\hbar$:
   $$L = m_e v r = n \hbar = n \frac{h}{2\pi} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$
3. **Frequency Condition (Quantum Jumps):** Emission or absorption of radiation takes place only when an electron jumps between permitted stationary states:
   $$hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = E_i - E_f$$

**(b)(i) Derivation of Energy Levels $E_n$:**  
For a nucleus of charge $+Ze$ and an electron of charge $-e$ moving at speed $v$ in a circle of radius $r$:  
1. Coulomb force equals centripetal force:
   $$\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Z e^2}{r^2} = \frac{m_e v^2}{r} \implies m_e v^2 = \frac{Z e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$
2. From angular momentum quantization, $v = \frac{n\hbar}{m_e r}$. Substituting into the centripetal equation:
   $$m_e \left(\frac{n\hbar}{m_e r}\right)^2 = \frac{Z e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} \implies \frac{n^2 \hbar^2}{m_e r} = \frac{Z e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \implies r_n = \frac{4\pi\epsilon_0 \hbar^2}{m_e Z e^2} n^2$$
3. Total mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energies:
   $$K = \frac{1}{2} m_e v^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Z e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_n}\right)$$
   $$U = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Z e^2}{r_n}$$
   $$E_n = K + U = -\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Z e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_n}\right) = -\frac{Z e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 r_n}$$
4. Substituting $r_n$:
   $$E_n = -\frac{Z e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0} \left(\frac{m_e Z e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 \hbar^2 n^2}\right) = -\frac{m_e Z^2 e^4}{32\pi^2 \epsilon_0^2 \hbar^2} \frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{m_e Z^2 e^4}{8\epsilon_0^2 h^2} \frac{1}{n^2} \quad \text{[Q.E.D.]}$$
   For Hydrogen ($Z=1$): $E_n = -\frac{13.6\text{ eV}}{n^2}$.

**(b)(ii) Transition Wavelength Formula:**  
$$\Delta E = E_i - E_f = \frac{m_e Z^2 e^4}{8\epsilon_0^2 h^2}\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right) = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$
$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = \left(\frac{m_e e^4}{8\epsilon_0^2 h^3 c}\right) Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right) = R_\infty Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$$
where $R_\infty = \frac{m_e e^4}{8\epsilon_0^2 h^3 c} \approx 1.097 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}$ is the Rydberg constant.

**(c) Pickering Series in $\text{He}^+$:**  
For singly ionized Helium ($\text{He}^+$), atomic number is $Z = 2$.  
Consider transitions ending at principal quantum number $n_f = 4$ from initial levels $n_i = m$ ($m = 5, 6, 7, \dots$):
$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_\infty (2^2) \left(\frac{1}{4^2} - \frac{1}{m^2}\right) = 4 R_\infty \left(\frac{1}{16} - \frac{1}{m^2}\right) = R_\infty \left(\frac{4}{16} - \frac{4}{m^2}\right) = R_\infty \left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{(m/2)^2}\right)$$
$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_\infty \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{(m/2)^2}\right)$$
This matches Pickering's empirical formula identically!
- **When $m$ is even** ($m = 2k$ where $k = 3, 4, 5, \dots$):
  $$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_\infty \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{k^2}\right)$$
  This is mathematically identical to the **Balmer series of Hydrogen** ($n_f = 2, n_i = k$), which caused Pickering to mistakenly conclude he had discovered a new form of stellar hydrogen.
- **When $m$ is odd** ($m = 5, 7, 9, \dots$):
  $(m/2)$ is a half-integer ($2.5, 3.5, 4.5, \dots$). These transitions generate distinct spectral lines that fall directly between the familiar hydrogen Balmer lines, proving they originate from $\text{He}^+$ ($Z=2$).

#### Problem 10: Radioactive Decay Kinetics & Carbon-14 Archaeological Dating
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 06; 2022/2023 Question 07; Lecture 08)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) State the radioactive decay law in terms of activity $A(t)$ and number of nuclei $N(t)$.  
(b) Derive the relationship between decay constant $\lambda$ and half-life $T_{1/2}$.  
(c) Briefly explain the atmospheric formation of Carbon-14, how it enters the food chain, and how it is utilized to date ancient organic artifacts.  
(d) A 1.00 g carbon sample recovered from an ancient archaeological excavation exhibits a beta activity of $0.050\text{ Bq}$. A 1.00 g carbon sample from modern living plant tissue exhibits an activity of $0.120\text{ Bq}$. Given that the half-life of Carbon-14 is $5730\text{ years}$:  
(i) Calculate the decay constant $\lambda$ of Carbon-14 in $\text{year}^{-1}$ and $\text{s}^{-1}$.  
(ii) Determine the age of the archaeological sample.  
(e) The radioactive isotope $^{226}_{88}\text{Ra}$ has a half-life of $1.60 \times 10^3\text{ years}$. If a sample initially contains $3.00 \times 10^{16}$ radium nuclei:  
(i) Find its initial activity $A_0$ in Becquerels.  
(ii) Calculate its decay rate after $2.00 \times 10^3\text{ years}$.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Radioactive Decay Law:**  
The rate of spontaneous nuclear disintegration is directly proportional to the number of parent nuclei $N$ present:
$$\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N \implies N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$
Activity $A(t)$ is defined as the disintegration rate:
$$A(t) = -\frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N(t) = \lambda N_0 e^{-\lambda t} = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$

**(b) Relationship between $\lambda$ and $T_{1/2}$:**  
At $t = T_{1/2}$, the remaining nuclei count drops to half of initial: $N(T_{1/2}) = \frac{N_0}{2}$.
$$\frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} \implies e^{\lambda T_{1/2}} = 2 \implies \lambda T_{1/2} = \ln 2 \implies T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$$

**(c) Carbon-14 Atmospheric Cycle & Dating:**  
1. **Formation:** High-energy cosmic ray neutrons bombard nitrogen-14 in the upper atmosphere:
   $$^{14}_7\text{N} + n \to {}^{14}_6\text{C} + p$$
2. **Assimilation:** $^{14}\text{C}$ rapidly oxidizes to $^{14}\text{CO}_2$, which is absorbed by autotrophic plants via photosynthesis and subsequently ingested by animals.
3. **Equilibrium:** During an organism's lifetime, constant carbon exchange maintains an equilibrium activity ratio of $\approx 0.23\text{ Bq/g}$ (or $\approx 15\text{ decays/min/g}$).
4. **Decay After Death:** Upon death, metabolic carbon intake ceases. The trapped $^{14}\text{C}$ disintegrates via $\beta^-$-emission with half-life $5730\text{ years}$:
   $$^{14}_6\text{C} \to {}^{14}_7\text{N} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$
5. **Dating:** Comparing residual specific activity $A$ against modern baseline $A_0$ gives artifact age $t = \frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(A_0/A)$.

**(d)(i) Decay Constant $\lambda$:**  
$$\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693147}{5730\text{ years}} = \mathbf{1.21 \times 10^{-4}\text{ year}^{-1}}$$
In $\text{s}^{-1}$ ($1\text{ year} \approx 3.1558 \times 10^7\text{ s}$):
$$\lambda = \frac{1.2097 \times 10^{-4}}{3.1558 \times 10^7\text{ s}} = \mathbf{3.83 \times 10^{-12}\text{ s}^{-1}}$$

**(d)(ii) Age of Archaeological Sample:**  
$$A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} \implies \frac{A_0}{A} = e^{\lambda t} \implies t = \frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A}\right)$$
$$t = \frac{5730\text{ years}}{\ln 2} \ln\left(\frac{0.120\text{ Bq}}{0.050\text{ Bq}}\right) = (8266.64) \times \ln(2.40)$$
$$t = 8266.64 \times 0.87547 = \mathbf{7.24 \times 10^3\text{ years}} \quad (\mathbf{7237\text{ years}})$$

**(e)(i) Initial Activity of Radium Sample:**  
Half-life of $^{226}\text{Ra}$: $T_{1/2} = 1.60 \times 10^3\text{ y} = 1600 \times 3.1558 \times 10^7\text{ s} \approx 5.049 \times 10^{10}\text{ s}$.
$$\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.69315}{5.049 \times 10^{10}\text{ s}} = 1.373 \times 10^{-11}\text{ s}^{-1}$$
Initial activity:
$$A_0 = \lambda N_0 = (1.373 \times 10^{-11}\text{ s}^{-1}) \times (3.00 \times 10^{16}\text{ nuclei}) = \mathbf{4.12 \times 10^5\text{ Bq}}$$

**(e)(ii) Activity after $t = 2.00 \times 10^3\text{ years}$:**  
Number of half-lives elapsed: $n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{2000}{1600} = 1.25$.
$$A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T_{1/2}} = (4.119 \times 10^5\text{ Bq}) \times (2)^{-1.25}$$
$$A(t) = (4.119 \times 10^5\text{ Bq}) \times 0.42045 = \mathbf{1.73 \times 10^5\text{ Bq}}$$

#### Problem 11: Nuclear Fission Reaction, Q-Value & Nuclear Reactor Output
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 06(b); Lecture 09)*

**Problem Statement:**  
In a nuclear fission reactor, the following induced thermal fission reaction occurs:
$$^{235}_{92}\text{U} + n \to {}^{141}_a\text{X} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + b\,n + Q$$
Given the atomic masses:
- $m(^{235}\text{U}) = 235.04393\text{ u}$
- $m(^{141}\text{X}) = 140.91441\text{ u}$
- $m(^{92}\text{Kr}) = 91.92615\text{ u}$
- $m_n = 1.00867\text{ u}$
- $m_p = 1.007825\text{ u}$
- Energy equivalent: $1\text{ u} = 931.5\text{ MeV}/c^2$  
(a) Determine the integer values of $a$ and $b$, and identify element $\text{X}$.  
(b) Calculate the mean binding energy per nucleon of $^{235}_{92}\text{U}$.  
(c) Calculate the $Q$-value (energy released) of this fission reaction in $\text{MeV}$.  
(d) If $1.00\text{ kg}$ of pure uranium-235 undergoes this fission reaction uniformly over the course of one year ($365.25\text{ days}$), calculate the thermal power output of the reactor in Megawatts ($\text{MW}$).

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Balancing Atomic and Mass Numbers:**  
- **Conservation of Atomic Number (Charge $Z$):**
  $$92 + 0 = a + 36 + b(0) \implies a = 92 - 36 = \mathbf{56}$$
  Element with $Z = 56$ is **Barium (Ba)** ($^{141}_{56}\text{Ba}$).
- **Conservation of Mass Number (Nucleons $A$):**
  $$235 + 1 = 141 + 92 + b(1) \implies 236 = 233 + b \implies b = 236 - 233 = \mathbf{3}$$
  Thus, $3$ fast neutrons are produced.

**(b) Mean Binding Energy per Nucleon of $^{235}_{92}\text{U}$:**  
- Composition: $Z = 92$ protons and $N = 235 - 92 = 143$ neutrons.
- Mass defect $\Delta m$:
  $$\Delta m = 92 m_p + 143 m_n - m(^{235}\text{U})$$
  $$\Delta m = 92(1.007825\text{ u}) + 143(1.008670\text{ u}) - 235.04393\text{ u}$$
  $$\Delta m = 92.71990\text{ u} + 144.23981\text{ u} - 235.04393\text{ u} = 236.95971\text{ u} - 235.04393\text{ u} = 1.91578\text{ u}$$
- Total binding energy:
  $$B = \Delta m \times 931.5\text{ MeV} = 1.91578 \times 931.5\text{ MeV} \approx 1784.55\text{ MeV}$$
- Mean binding energy per nucleon:
  $$\bar{B} = \frac{B}{A} = \frac{1784.55\text{ MeV}}{235} = \mathbf{7.59\text{ MeV/nucleon}}$$

**(c) Q-Value Calculation:**  
Reactant mass:
$$M_{\text{initial}} = m(^{235}\text{U}) + m_n = 235.04393\text{ u} + 1.00867\text{ u} = 236.05260\text{ u}$$
Product mass:
$$M_{\text{final}} = m(^{141}\text{Ba}) + m(^{92}\text{Kr}) + 3 m_n = 140.91441\text{ u} + 91.92615\text{ u} + 3(1.00867\text{ u})$$
$$M_{\text{final}} = 232.84056\text{ u} + 3.02601\text{ u} = 235.86657\text{ u}$$
Mass defect in reaction:
$$\Delta M = M_{\text{initial}} - M_{\text{final}} = 236.05260\text{ u} - 235.86657\text{ u} = 0.18603\text{ u}$$
Q-value:
$$Q = \Delta M \times 931.5\text{ MeV} = 0.18603 \times 931.5\text{ MeV} = \mathbf{173.29\text{ MeV}}$$
*(In Joules: $Q = 173.287 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13}\text{ J} = 2.776 \times 10^{-11}\text{ J per fission event}$.)*

**(d) Reactor Power Output for $1.00\text{ kg U-235 per year}$:**  
- Molar mass of $^{235}\text{U} \approx 235.04\text{ g/mol} = 0.23504\text{ kg/mol}$.
- Number of $^{235}\text{U}$ nuclei in $1.00\text{ kg}$:
  $$N_{\text{fissions}} = \frac{m}{M_{\text{mol}}} N_A = \frac{1.00\text{ kg}}{0.235044\text{ kg/mol}} \times (6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}) \approx 2.562 \times 10^{24}\text{ fissions}$$
- Total energy released in one year:
  $$E_{\text{total}} = N_{\text{fissions}} \times Q = (2.562 \times 10^{24}) \times (2.776 \times 10^{-11}\text{ J}) \approx 7.112 \times 10^{13}\text{ J}$$
- Time elapsed:
  $$t = 1\text{ year} = 365.25 \times 24 \times 3600\text{ s} \approx 3.1558 \times 10^7\text{ s}$$
- Thermal power output:
  $$P = \frac{E_{\text{total}}}{t} = \frac{7.112 \times 10^{13}\text{ J}}{3.1558 \times 10^7\text{ s}} \approx 2.254 \times 10^6\text{ W} = \mathbf{2.25\text{ MW}}$$

#### Problem 12: Primary vs Secondary Chemical Bonding & Potential Energy Curve Features
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 07; 2022/2023 Question 07; Handwritten Notes)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) (i) Name three primary bond types and three secondary bond types found in condensed matter.  
(ii) Briefly explain the fundamental physical difference between primary bonds and secondary bonds.  
(b) (i) State three macroscopic physical properties of bulk materials that can be understood directly using the interatomic potential energy curve $U(r)$ of a diatomic system.  
(ii) Explain clearly how specific geometric and mathematical features of the potential energy curve $U(r)$ govern each of the three physical properties stated above.  
(c) The potential energy curves as a function of internuclear distance $r$ for three diatomic molecules $X_2, Y_2,$ and $Z_2$ are plotted on the same axes:
- $X_2$ exhibits a minimum located at the smallest internuclear distance ($r_0 \approx 0.74\text{ \AA}$) with moderate well depth ($\sim 4.5\text{ eV}$).
- $Z_2$ exhibits the deepest potential energy well ($E_0 \approx 9.8\text{ eV}$) at an intermediate internuclear separation ($r_0 \approx 1.10\text{ \AA}$).
- $Y_2$ exhibits an intermediate well depth ($\sim 5.1\text{ eV}$) and internuclear distance ($r_0 \approx 1.21\text{ \AA}$).  
Identify which curve corresponds to $\text{H}_2, \text{N}_2,$ and $\text{O}_2$, giving explicit physical reasons.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a)(i) Bond Classifications:**  
- **Three Primary Bonds:**
  1. *Ionic bonding* (e.g., $\text{NaCl}, \text{MgO}$)
  2. *Covalent bonding* (e.g., $\text{Diamond}, \text{Si}, \text{Cl}_2$)
  3. *Metallic bonding* (e.g., $\text{Al}, \text{Cu}, \text{Fe}$)
- **Three Secondary Bonds (van der Waals forces):**
  1. *Dipole-dipole interactions / Keesom forces* (between permanent dipoles)
  2. *Dipole-induced dipole interactions / Debye forces* (permanent dipole inducing a dipole in a polarizable molecule)
  3. *London dispersion forces* (instantaneous quantum fluctuating dipoles; universal to all atoms)  
  *(Alternative valid secondary bond: Hydrogen bonding, e.g., in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or polymer chains).*

**(a)(ii) Fundamental Difference:**  
- **Primary Bonds:** Strong bonds (bond energies $\sim 100 - 1000\text{ kJ/mol}$) formed by the transfer (ionic), sharing (covalent), or delocalization (metallic) of valence electrons. They act at short interatomic distances and hold atoms together within a molecule or crystal lattice.
- **Secondary Bonds:** Much weaker intermolecular attractions (energies $\sim 1 - 50\text{ kJ/mol}$) arising from electrostatic attractions between permanent or temporary fluctuating electric dipoles. They involve **no valence electron transfer or orbital sharing**, acting predominantly between separate molecules or polymer chains.

**(b)(i) Three Physical Properties:**  
1. **Melting and Boiling Temperatures ($T_m, T_b$)**
2. **Elastic Modulus (Young's Modulus $E$) / Material Stiffness**
3. **Coefficient of Thermal Expansion ($\alpha$)**

**(b)(ii) Governed by Features of $U(r)$ Curve:**  
1. **Melting / Boiling Temperature $\longleftrightarrow$ Depth of the Potential Well ($E_0 = -U(r_0)$):**  
   The depth of the potential well represents the bond dissociation energy—the energy required to pull atoms from their equilibrium separation $r_0$ to infinity. A deeper well means stronger atomic cohesion; consequently, much higher thermal kinetic energy ($k_B T$) is required to break the lattice bonds and cause melting/vaporization. Materials with deep potential wells (e.g., diamond, tungsten) have extremely high melting points.
2. **Elastic Modulus / Stiffness $\longleftrightarrow$ Curvature of the Well Minimum ($\left.\frac{d^2U}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0}$):**  
   Around the equilibrium separation $r_0$, the potential energy can be expanded in a Taylor series:
   $$U(r) \approx U(r_0) + \left.\frac{dU}{dr}\right|_{r_0}(r - r_0) + \frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{d^2U}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0}(r - r_0)^2 = -E_0 + \frac{1}{2}k(r - r_0)^2$$
   The restoring force against atomic displacement is $F = -k(r - r_0)$, where the effective interatomic spring constant is $k = \left.\frac{d^2U}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0}$. The macroscopic Young's modulus scales directly as $E \approx \frac{k}{r_0} \propto \left.\frac{d^2U}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0}$. A steeper, narrower potential well possesses a larger second derivative, conferring higher stiffness and resistance to elastic strain.
3. **Thermal Expansion $\longleftrightarrow$ Asymmetry / Anharmonicity of the Potential Well:**  
   At $0\text{ K}$, the atoms sit at the bottom of the well ($r = r_0$). As temperature $T$ rises, thermal vibrational energy increases, causing the atoms to oscillate between turning points $r_{\min}$ and $r_{\max}$. Because the repulsive wall ($r < r_0$) is extremely steep (due to Pauli exclusion) while the attractive branch ($r > r_0$) rises more gently, the potential well is **asymmetric (anharmonic)**. As a result, the midpoint of oscillation $\langle r \rangle$ shifts toward larger distances as vibrational energy increases ($\frac{d\langle r \rangle}{dT} > 0$). A highly asymmetric, shallow well yields a large coefficient of thermal expansion $\alpha$; a deep, highly symmetric well yields very low thermal expansion.

**(c) Identification of $X_2, Y_2, Z_2$:**  
- **$X_2$ is Hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$):** Hydrogen consists of single 1s valence electrons. It has the smallest covalent atomic radius, resulting in the smallest equilibrium bond length ($r_0 = 0.74\text{ \AA}$).
- **$Z_2$ is Nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$):** Nitrogen atoms form a covalent **triple bond** ($\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$), which is exceptionally strong. It possesses the greatest bond dissociation energy ($E_0 \approx 9.79\text{ eV} = 945\text{ kJ/mol}$), corresponding to the deepest potential energy well.
- **$Y_2$ is Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$):** Oxygen atoms form a covalent **double bond** ($\text{O}=\text{O}$), with intermediate bond dissociation energy ($E_0 \approx 5.12\text{ eV} = 498\text{ kJ/mol}$) and intermediate bond length ($r_0 = 1.21\text{ \AA}$).

#### Problem 13: Lennard-Jones (6-12) Potential Derivations & Spring Constant
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 07(c); 2022/2023 Question 07(b); 2015/2016 Question 08)*

**Problem Statement:**  
The Lennard-Jones interatomic potential between two atoms in a lattice can be written as:
$$V(r) = 4\epsilon \left[ \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^6 \right]$$
where $\epsilon$ and $\sigma$ are positive constants, and $r$ is the internuclear separation.  
(a) Identify the attractive and repulsive terms in the potential and comment on their physical origins.  
(b) Show that the parameter $\sigma$ is related to the equilibrium separation $r_0$ by:
$$\sigma = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1/6} r_0 \quad \text{or} \quad r_0 = 2^{1/6} \sigma$$
(c) For Nickel ($\text{Ni}$), the parameter $\sigma$ has a value of $1.5808\text{ \AA}$. Calculate the equilibrium separation $r_0$ of the Nickel atoms in the lattice.  
(d) Derive an expression for the effective interatomic spring constant $k$ at equilibrium in terms of $\epsilon$ and $r_0$.  
(e) If the potential is expressed in the alternative form $V(r) = \frac{A}{r^{12}} - \frac{B}{r^6}$ with $A = 0.755 \times 10^{-119}\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}^{12}$ and $B = 0.853 \times 10^{-59}\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}^6$, calculate:  
(i) The equilibrium separation $r_0$.  
(ii) The binding energy (well depth $E_0$).  
(iii) The effective spring constant $k$ in $\text{N/m}$.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Terms and Physical Origins:**  
- **Repulsive Potential:** $V_{\text{rep}}(r) = +4\epsilon \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} = \frac{A}{r^{12}}$.  
  *Origin:* Operates at very short interatomic distances where electron clouds overlap. It arises from the **Pauli Exclusion Principle**, which forbids electrons with identical quantum states from occupying the same space, creating powerful quantum electrostatic repulsion between overlapping closed shells.
- **Attractive Potential:** $V_{\text{att}}(r) = -4\epsilon \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^6 = -\frac{B}{r^6}$.  
  *Origin:* Operates at longer ranges; represents **London dispersion forces** (induced dipole-induced dipole interactions) caused by quantum fluctuations in electron cloud distributions.

**(b) Derivation of Equilibrium Separation $r_0$:**  
At mechanical equilibrium, the net interatomic force is zero:
$$\vec{F} = -\frac{dV}{dr}\hat{r} = 0 \implies \left.\frac{dV}{dr}\right|_{r=r_0} = 0$$
Differentiating $V(r) = 4\epsilon [\sigma^{12} r^{-12} - \sigma^6 r^{-6}]$:
$$\frac{dV}{dr} = 4\epsilon \left[ -12 \sigma^{12} r^{-13} - (-6) \sigma^6 r^{-7} \right] = 4\epsilon \left[ -\frac{12\sigma^{12}}{r^{13}} + \frac{6\sigma^6}{r^7} \right]$$
Setting $\left.\frac{dV}{dr}\right|_{r_0} = 0$:
$$-\frac{12\sigma^{12}}{r_0^{13}} + \frac{6\sigma^6}{r_0^7} = 0 \implies \frac{12\sigma^{12}}{r_0^{13}} = \frac{6\sigma^6}{r_0^7}$$
$$2 \frac{\sigma^6}{r_0^6} = 1 \implies r_0^6 = 2\sigma^6 \implies r_0 = 2^{1/6} \sigma \quad \text{and} \quad \sigma = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1/6} r_0 \quad \text{[Q.E.D.]}$$

**(c) Calculation for Nickel ($\text{Ni}$):**  
Given $\sigma = 1.5808\text{ \AA}$:
$$r_0 = 2^{1/6} \sigma = (1.122462) \times (1.5808\text{ \AA}) = \mathbf{1.7744\text{ \AA}} = \mathbf{1.77 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}}$$

**(d) Derivation of Effective Spring Constant $k$:**  
$$k = \left.\frac{d^2V}{dr^2}\right|_{r=r_0}$$
Differentiating $\frac{dV}{dr} = 4\epsilon [-12\sigma^{12} r^{-13} + 6\sigma^6 r^{-7}]$:
$$\frac{d^2V}{dr^2} = 4\epsilon \left[ (-12)(-13)\sigma^{12} r^{-14} + 6(-7)\sigma^6 r^{-8} \right] = 4\epsilon \left[ \frac{156\sigma^{12}}{r^{14}} - \frac{42\sigma^6}{r^8} \right]$$
At $r = r_0$, substitute $\left(\frac{\sigma}{r_0}\right)^6 = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\left(\frac{\sigma}{r_0}\right)^{12} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}$:
$$\left.\frac{d^2V}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0} = \frac{4\epsilon}{r_0^2} \left[ 156\left(\frac{\sigma}{r_0}\right)^{12} - 42\left(\frac{\sigma}{r_0}\right)^6 \right] = \frac{4\epsilon}{r_0^2} \left[ 156\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) - 42\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \right]$$
$$\left.\frac{d^2V}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0} = \frac{4\epsilon}{r_0^2} [39 - 21] = \frac{4\epsilon}{r_0^2} [18] = \mathbf{\frac{72\epsilon}{r_0^2}} \quad \text{[Q.E.D.]}$$

**(e) Alternative Form $V(r) = \frac{A}{r^{12}} - \frac{B}{r^6}$:**  
Comparing coefficients: $A = 4\epsilon \sigma^{12}$ and $B = 4\epsilon \sigma^6$.
1. **Equilibrium separation $r_0$:**
   $$\frac{dV}{dr} = -\frac{12A}{r^{13}} + \frac{6B}{r^7} = 0 \implies r_0^6 = \frac{2A}{B} \implies r_0 = \left(\frac{2A}{B}\right)^{1/6}$$
   $$r_0 = \left(\frac{2 \times 0.755 \times 10^{-119}\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}^{12}}{0.853 \times 10^{-59}\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}^6}\right)^{1/6} = (1.7702 \times 10^{-60})^{1/6} = \mathbf{1.10 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{1.10\text{ \AA}}$$
2. **Binding Energy (Well Depth $E_0$):**
   $$E_0 = -V(r_0) = -\left[\frac{A}{r_0^{12}} - \frac{B}{r_0^6}\right] = -\left[\frac{A}{(2A/B)^2} - \frac{B}{(2A/B)}\right] = -\left[\frac{B^2}{4A} - \frac{B^2}{2A}\right] = \frac{B^2}{4A}$$
   $$E_0 = \frac{(0.853 \times 10^{-59})^2}{4 \times (0.755 \times 10^{-119})} = \frac{7.276 \times 10^{-119}}{3.020 \times 10^{-119}} \approx \mathbf{2.41\text{ eV}} \quad (= 3.86 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J})$$
3. **Effective Spring Constant $k$:**
   $$k = \left.\frac{d^2V}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0} = \frac{156 A}{r_0^{14}} - \frac{42 B}{r_0^8} = \frac{156 A (B/2A)}{r_0^8} - \frac{42 B}{r_0^8} = \frac{78 B - 42 B}{r_0^8} = \frac{36 B}{r_0^8}$$
   $$k = \frac{36 \times (0.853 \times 10^{-59}\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}^6)}{(1.10 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m})^8} = \frac{3.0708 \times 10^{-58}}{2.1436 \times 10^{-80}} = \mathbf{14.3\text{ N/m}}$$

#### Problem 14: Diatomic Molecular Rotational Spectroscopy & Chlorine Isotope Doublets
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 08; 2022/2023 Question 08; Handwritten Notes)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) The bond length of the homonuclear diatomic chlorine molecule $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$ is $r_0 = 1.98\text{ \AA} = 1.98 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$. The atomic mass of the $^{35}\text{Cl}$ isotope is $34.968853\text{ u}$ ($1\text{ u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$).  
(i) Calculate the reduced mass $\mu$ and the moment of inertia $I$ of the $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$ molecule.  
(ii) Calculate the quantized rotational energies of the first excited rotational state ($J = 1$) and the second excited rotational state ($J = 2$) in Joules.  
(iii) Find the wavelength $\lambda$ and frequency of the radiation emitted during the transition from $J = 2$ to $J = 1$, and state the region of the electromagnetic spectrum to which it belongs.  
(iv) Now consider the isotopic molecule $^{37}\text{Cl}_2$ with the identical bond length $r_0 = 1.98\text{ \AA}$, where the atomic mass of $^{37}\text{Cl}$ is $36.965903\text{ u}$. Qualitatively and quantitatively compare the wavelength emitted in the $J = 2 \to J = 1$ transition with that of $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$.  
(b) In a heteronuclear diatomic molecule $\text{H}^{35}\text{Cl}$, the equilibrium bond length is $r_0 = 0.12746\text{ nm}$. Atomic masses: $m(\text{H}) = 1.007825\text{ u}$, $m(^{35}\text{Cl}) = 34.968853\text{ u}$, and $m(^{37}\text{Cl}) = 36.965903\text{ u}$.  
(i) Calculate the longest wavelength in the pure rotational spectrum of $\text{H}^{35}\text{Cl}$.  
(ii) Repeat for $\text{H}^{37}\text{Cl}$.  
(iii) Explain why each line in the microwave rotational spectrum of natural $\text{HCl}$ gas appears as a closely spaced doublet, and calculate the wavelength separation $\Delta \lambda$ of the doublet for the longest wavelength transition.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a)(i) Reduced Mass & Moment of Inertia for $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$:**  
For a homonuclear diatomic molecule ($m_1 = m_2 = m$):
$$\mu = \frac{m \cdot m}{m + m} = \frac{m}{2} = \frac{34.968853 \times (1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg})}{2} = \mathbf{2.903 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}}$$
Moment of inertia:
$$I = \mu r_0^2 = (2.9032 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}) \times (1.98 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m})^2 = \mathbf{1.138 \times 10^{-45}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2}$$

**(a)(ii) Rotational Energy Levels ($J=1$ and $J=2$):**  
Rotational energy of a rigid rotor:
$$E_J = \frac{\hbar^2}{2I} J(J + 1) = \frac{h^2}{8\pi^2 I} J(J + 1)$$
Prefactor:
$$\frac{\hbar^2}{2I} = \frac{(1.0546 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s})^2}{2 \times (1.138 \times 10^{-45}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2)} = \frac{1.1122 \times 10^{-68}}{2.276 \times 10^{-45}} \approx 4.887 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J}$$
- For $J = 1$:
  $$E_1 = \left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2I}\right) (1)(2) = 2 \times (4.887 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J}) = \mathbf{9.77 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J}}$$
- For $J = 2$:
  $$E_2 = \left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2I}\right) (2)(3) = 6 \times (4.887 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J}) = \mathbf{2.93 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J}}$$

**(a)(iii) Transition $J = 2 \to 1$ Radiation:**  
$$\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = (2.932 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J}) - (0.977 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J}) = 1.955 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J}$$
Wavelength:
$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}) \times (3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s})}{1.955 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J}} = \frac{1.989 \times 10^{-25}}{1.955 \times 10^{-23}} = \mathbf{1.017 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{1.02\text{ cm}}$$
Frequency:
$$f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8}{0.01017} = \mathbf{2.95 \times 10^{10}\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{29.5\text{ GHz}}$$
*Spectral Region:* **Microwave** region.

**(a)(iv) Isotope Comparison with $^{37}\text{Cl}_2$:**  
- Atomic mass of $^{37}\text{Cl} = 36.9659\text{ u} > 34.9689\text{ u}$.
- Since bond length $r_0$ is chemically identical:
  $$\mu' = \frac{m'}{2} > \mu \implies I' = \mu' r_0^2 > I$$
- Since $E_J \propto \frac{1}{I}$, all energy levels are compressed downward.
- Transition energy: $\Delta E' = \frac{\hbar^2}{I'} (2) < \Delta E$.
- Emitted wavelength:
  $$\lambda' = \frac{hc}{\Delta E'} \propto I \propto \mu$$
  $$\frac{\lambda'}{\lambda} = \frac{\mu'}{\mu} = \frac{36.965903}{34.968853} \approx 1.0571 \implies \lambda' \approx 1.0571 \times 1.017\text{ cm} = \mathbf{1.075\text{ cm}}$$
  *Conclusion:* Emitted radiation shifts to a **longer wavelength (lower frequency)**.

**(b)(i) Longest Wavelength for $\text{H}^{35}\text{Cl}$:**  
Longest rotational wavelength corresponds to lowest energy transition: $J = 1 \to 0$.
$$\Delta E = E_1 - E_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{I} (1) = \frac{h^2}{4\pi^2 I} \implies \lambda_{\max} = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{4\pi^2 c I}{h}$$
Reduced mass of $\text{H}^{35}\text{Cl}$:
$$\mu_1 = \frac{m_{\text{H}} \cdot m_{35}}{m_{\text{H}} + m_{35}} = \frac{1.007825 \times 34.968853}{1.007825 + 34.968853} \times (1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg})$$
$$\mu_1 = (0.979606\text{ u}) \times (1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}) = 1.6266 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$$
Moment of inertia:
$$I_1 = \mu_1 r_0^2 = (1.6266 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}) \times (0.12746 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m})^2 = 2.6425 \times 10^{-47}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$$
Longest wavelength:
$$\lambda_1 = \frac{4\pi^2 (3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}) (2.6425 \times 10^{-47}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2)}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}} = \mathbf{4.72 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{472\ \mu\text{m}} = \mathbf{0.472\text{ mm}}$$

**(b)(ii) Longest Wavelength for $\text{H}^{37}\text{Cl}$:**  
$$\mu_2 = \frac{1.007825 \times 36.965903}{1.007825 + 36.965903} \times (1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}) = (0.981068\text{ u}) \times (1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}) = 1.6291 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$$
$$I_2 = \mu_2 r_0^2 = (1.6291 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}) \times (0.12746 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m})^2 = 2.6465 \times 10^{-47}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$$
$$\lambda_2 = \frac{4\pi^2 c I_2}{h} = \mathbf{4.73 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{473\ \mu\text{m}} = \mathbf{0.473\text{ mm}}$$

**(b)(iii) Doublet Splitting Explanation & $\Delta \lambda$:**  
Natural terrestrial chlorine occurs as an isotopic mixture of approximately $75.8\%\ {}^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $24.2\%\ {}^{37}\text{Cl}$ (a $3:1$ natural ratio). Every rotational transition therefore occurs simultaneously in two distinct molecular species ($\text{H}^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $\text{H}^{37}\text{Cl}$) present in the gas sample. Because $\mu_2 > \mu_1$, the transition energies differ slightly, splitting every single rotational line into a closely spaced doublet whose peak intensities reflect the $3:1$ isotopic abundance.
$$\Delta \lambda = \lambda_2 - \lambda_1 = 4.730 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} - 4.723 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = \mathbf{0.70\ \mu\text{m}} = \mathbf{7.0 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}}$$

#### Problem 15: Harmonic Oscillator Vibrational Energy & Classical Amplitude
*(Source: End Semester Examination 2023/2024 Question 08(b)–(c); Handwritten Notes)*

**Problem Statement:**  
(a) Write down an expression for the quantized vibrational energy of the $v$-th state of a diatomic molecule, and obtain an expression for the energy difference between two consecutive vibrational levels. Define all symbols used.  
(b) The effective interatomic spring constant of the $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$ molecule is $k = 323\text{ N/m}$. The reduced mass is $\mu = 2.9024 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}$.  
(i) Calculate the fundamental vibrational frequency $f_0$ and the level spacing $\Delta E$.  
(ii) Assuming the vibrational motion obeys classical simple harmonic motion at the energy of the quantum state, find the classical amplitude of oscillation $A_1$ when the molecule is in its first excited vibrational state ($v = 1$).  
(c) The interatomic spring constant of the Iodine molecule $\text{I}_2$ is $k = 169\text{ N/m}$, and the molecular weight of $\text{I}_2$ is $253.81\text{ u}$ ($\mu = 1.053 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg}$). When comparing $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$ with $\text{I}_2$, which molecule will absorb higher energy photons during transitions between consecutive vibrational levels? Fully justify your answer mathematically.

---

**Step-by-Step Model Solution:**

**(a) Vibrational Energy & Level Spacing:**  
For a diatomic molecule modeled as a quantum simple harmonic oscillator:
$$E_v = \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right) \hbar \omega_0 = \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right) h f_0 = \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right) \frac{h}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}} \quad (v = 0, 1, 2, \dots)$$
where:
- $v$: Vibrational quantum number ($0, 1, 2, \dots$).
- $h$: Planck's constant.
- $f_0$: Classical natural frequency of vibration ($f_0 = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}}$).
- $k$: Effective interatomic spring constant (force constant).
- $\mu$: Reduced mass of the diatomic system ($\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$).

The energy difference between two consecutive vibrational levels ($v \to v+1$):
$$\Delta E = E_{v+1} - E_v = \left[(v + 1 + 1/2) - (v + 1/2)\right] h f_0 = \mathbf{h f_0} = \mathbf{\frac{h}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}}}$$
*(Crucial Remark: The spacing is completely independent of the quantum number $v$—all adjacent vibrational levels in a harmonic oscillator are equally spaced!)*

**(b)(i) Frequency $f_0$ and Level Spacing $\Delta E$ for $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$:**  
$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{323\text{ N/m}}{2.9024 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{1.1129 \times 10^{28}} = \frac{1.0549 \times 10^{14}}{2\pi} = \mathbf{1.679 \times 10^{13}\text{ Hz}}$$
Level spacing:
$$\Delta E = h f_0 = (6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}) \times (1.679 \times 10^{13}\text{ s}^{-1}) = \mathbf{1.113 \times 10^{-20}\text{ J}} = \mathbf{0.0695\text{ eV}}$$

**(b)(ii) Classical Amplitude in First Excited State ($v = 1$):**  
Energy of state $v = 1$:
$$E_1 = \left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\right) h f_0 = \frac{3}{2} \Delta E = \frac{3}{2} \times (1.113 \times 10^{-20}\text{ J}) = 1.670 \times 10^{-20}\text{ J}$$
In a classical harmonic oscillator, total energy is equal to maximum potential energy at maximum amplitude $A$:
$$E_1 = \frac{1}{2} k A^2 \implies A = \sqrt{\frac{2 E_1}{k}}$$
$$A = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times (1.670 \times 10^{-20}\text{ J})}{323\text{ N/m}}} = \sqrt{1.034 \times 10^{-22}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{1.017 \times 10^{-11}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{0.1017\text{ \AA}}$$
*(Note: This represents only about $5.1\%$ of the total equilibrium bond length $r_0 = 1.98\text{ \AA}$, justifying the small-oscillation harmonic approximation!)*

**(c) Absorption Energy Comparison ($^{35}\text{Cl}_2$ vs $\text{I}_2$):**  
The energy of the absorbed photon during a $\Delta v = +1$ vibrational transition is:
$$\Delta E_{\text{absorbed}} = \hbar \omega_0 = \frac{h}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}} \implies \Delta E \propto \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}}$$
Let us compare the factors for each molecule:
- **For $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$:**
  $$\frac{k_{\text{Cl}}}{\mu_{\text{Cl}}} = \frac{323\text{ N/m}}{2.9024 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}} \approx 1.113 \times 10^{28}\text{ s}^{-2}$$
  $$\sqrt{\frac{k_{\text{Cl}}}{\mu_{\text{Cl}}}} \approx 1.055 \times 10^{14}\text{ rad/s}$$
- **For $\text{I}_2$:**  
  Reduced mass $\mu_{\text{I}} = \frac{m_{\text{I}}}{2} = \frac{126.905 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}}{2} \approx 1.0536 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg}$.
  $$\frac{k_{\text{I}}}{\mu_{\text{I}}} = \frac{169\text{ N/m}}{1.0536 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg}} \approx 1.604 \times 10^{27}\text{ s}^{-2}$$
  $$\sqrt{\frac{k_{\text{I}}}{\mu_{\text{I}}}} \approx 4.005 \times 10^{13}\text{ rad/s}$$
- **Ratio of Transition Energies:**
  $$\frac{\Delta E(\text{Cl}_2)}{\Delta E(\text{I}_2)} = \sqrt{\frac{k_{\text{Cl}}}{k_{\text{I}}} \cdot \frac{\mu_{\text{I}}}{\mu_{\text{Cl}}}} = \sqrt{\frac{323}{169} \times \frac{1.0536 \times 10^{-25}}{2.9024 \times 10^{-26}}} = \sqrt{1.911 \times 3.630} = \sqrt{6.937} \approx 2.63$$

**Conclusion:**  
$^{35}\text{Cl}_2$ absorbs significantly **higher energy photons** (approximately $2.63\times$ higher energy, meaning higher frequency and shorter infrared wavelength). This is because $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$ possesses both a substantially **stronger chemical bond / stiffer spring constant** ($k_{\text{Cl}} = 323\text{ N/m} > k_{\text{I}} = 169\text{ N/m}$) and a much **lighter reduced atomic mass** ($\mu_{\text{Cl}} \ll \mu_{\text{I}}$).

## 5. Common Pitfalls & Exam Traps

### 5.1 Electromagnetism (Module A) Pitfalls
1. **Electric Dipole Vector Direction Trap:**  
   - *Error:* Students often draw the electric dipole moment $\vec{p}$ from positive to negative (the convention sometimes used in introductory chemistry).  
   - *Correction:* In physics, the dipole moment vector is **strictly defined from negative charge to positive charge** ($\vec{p} = q \cdot 2\vec{a}$ directed $-q \to +q$). Writing the reverse flips the signs of torque $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$ and potential energy $U = -\vec{p}\cdot\vec{E}$.
2. **Gauss's Law End-Cap Flux Neglect:**  
   - *Error:* Omitting explicit mathematical justification for the end caps of a Gaussian cylinder.  
   - *Correction:* Always state clearly: $\vec{E} \perp d\vec{A}$ on flat end caps $\implies \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = 0$. Flux passes solely through the curved mantle surface: $\Phi = E(2\pi r l)$.
3. **Conducting vs Non-Conducting Cylinders:**  
   - *Error:* Writing $E = 0$ inside all cylinders.  
   - *Correction:* Inside a **conducting** cylinder at electrostatic equilibrium, $E = 0$ because free charges migrate exclusively to the outer surface. However, inside a **solid non-conducting** cylinder with uniform volume charge density $\rho$, charge is trapped throughout the volume, yielding a linear field: $E(r) = \frac{\lambda r}{2\pi \epsilon_0 a^2} \propto r$.
4. **Orbiting Electron Speed Invariance:**  
   - *Error:* Assuming that an electron orbiting closer to a charged line moves faster, as in gravitational Keplerian orbits ($v \propto 1/\sqrt{r}$).  
   - *Correction:* For a line charge, electric force scales as $1/r$, matching the $v^2/r$ centripetal acceleration requirement. Hence, tangential orbital speed $v = \sqrt{\frac{e\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 m_e}}$ is **independent of radius $r$**! Only orbital period $T \propto r$ changes with distance.
5. **Capacitor Dielectric Disconnection vs Connection:**  
   - *Error:* Confusing constant charge ($q = \text{constant}$) with constant voltage ($V = \text{constant}$).  
   - *Correction:* If the battery is **disconnected** before slab insertion, free charge $q$ is trapped and conserved; voltage drops to $V = E_0(d-b) + \frac{E_0}{\kappa}b$. If the battery remains **connected**, voltage $V_0$ is held constant, and charge increases ($q = C V_0$).
6. **Non-Uniform Current Density Integration in Ampère's Law:**  
   - *Error:* Writing $I_{\text{enc}} = J(r) \cdot \pi r^2$ when current density is non-uniform.  
   - *Correction:* You MUST integrate over differential annular rings: $I_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r J(r') (2\pi r' dr')$. Multiplying $J(r)$ directly by area loses all integration marks.
7. **Magnetic Work Fallacy:**  
   - *Error:* Claiming that a magnetic field accelerates a charged particle or increases its kinetic energy.  
   - *Correction:* The magnetic Lorentz force $\vec{F}_B = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$ is always normal to velocity ($\vec{F}_B \cdot \vec{v} \equiv 0$). It does **zero work** ($W = 0$) and alters only direction, never speed or kinetic energy.
8. **Cyclotron Transmission Boundary Trap:**  
   - *Error:* Assuming a charged particle exits the opposite boundary when field width $d > R$.  
   - *Correction:* When $d \ge R = \frac{mv}{qB}$, the particle completes a quarter-turn ($\theta = 90^\circ$ at $d=R$) or a full semicircle ($\theta = 180^\circ$ for $d \ge 2R$), emerging from the **same side** it entered.

### 5.2 Modern & Nuclear Physics (Module B) Pitfalls
1. **Temperature Unit Trap in Radiation Laws:**  
   - *Error:* Inserting temperatures in Celsius into the Stefan-Boltzmann law ($P \propto T^4$) or Wien's displacement law ($\lambda_m T = C_W$).  
   - *Correction:* Temperatures MUST be converted to absolute Kelvin ($T\text{ [K]} = T\text{ [}^\circ\text{C]} + 273.15$). Failing to convert produces catastrophic fourth-power errors.
2. **Work Function Unit Inconsistency:**  
   - *Error:* Subtracting work function $\Phi$ in $\text{eV}$ directly from $hf$ in Joules without conversion.  
   - *Correction:* Always match units before subtraction: either convert $hf$ to $\text{eV}$ by dividing by $1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J/eV}$, or convert $\Phi$ to Joules by multiplying by $1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J/eV}$.
3. **Stopping Potential Definition:**  
   - *Error:* Stating stopping potential in units of energy ($\text{eV}$ or Joules).  
   - *Correction:* Stopping potential $V_s$ is an electrical potential difference measured in **Volts ($\text{V}$)**. While maximum kinetic energy is $K_{\max} = 1.11\text{ eV}$, stopping potential is $V_s = 1.11\text{ V}$.
4. **Compton Scattering Radiation Requirement:**  
   - *Error:* Stating that Compton scattering can be observed using visible light.  
   - *Correction:* Compton shift $\Delta \lambda = \lambda_c(1 - \cos\theta) \le 4.85\text{ pm}$. For visible light ($\lambda \approx 500\text{ nm}$), $\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} \approx 0.001\%$, which is undetectable. For X-rays ($\lambda \approx 0.1\text{ nm}$), $\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} \approx 5\%$, yielding prominent, easily measurable shifts.
5. **Hydrogen-like Ion Atomic Number $Z$:**  
   - *Error:* Using $Z = 1$ in the Bohr energy formula for $\text{He}^+$ or $\text{Li}^{2+}$.  
   - *Correction:* Energy levels scale as $Z^2$: $E_n = -13.6 \frac{Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV}$. For $\text{He}^+$, $Z = 2 \implies E_1 = -54.4\text{ eV}$. Radius scales inversely: $r_n = \frac{n^2}{Z} a_0$.
6. **Activity Unit & Time Base Alignment:**  
   - *Error:* Calculating activity $A = \lambda N$ using $\lambda$ expressed in $\text{year}^{-1}$ or $\text{day}^{-1}$ while expecting the answer in Becquerels.  
   - *Correction:* The Becquerel is defined strictly as **one decay per second** ($\text{s}^{-1}$). When calculating activity in $\text{Bq}$, the decay constant $\lambda$ MUST be in $\text{s}^{-1}$ ($\lambda_{\text{s}^{-1}} = \frac{\lambda_{\text{year}^{-1}}}{3.156 \times 10^7}$).
7. **Mass Defect Calculation & Binding Energy:**  
   - *Error:* Confusing integer mass number $A$ with accurate isotopic mass $m$ in $\text{u}$.  
   - *Correction:* Mass defect requires exact atomic masses to 5 or 6 decimal places (e.g., $m(^{235}\text{U}) = 235.04393\text{ u}$). Rounding masses to whole integers results in a calculated mass defect of zero!

### 5.3 Molecules & Solids (Module C) Pitfalls
1. **Lennard-Jones Parameter Relationship Inversion:**  
   - *Error:* Writing $\sigma = 2^{1/6} r_0$ instead of $r_0 = 2^{1/6} \sigma$.  
   - *Correction:* The parameter $\sigma$ represents the finite separation distance where the potential crosses zero ($V(\sigma) = 0$). The equilibrium minimum $r_0$ lies further out where attractive and repulsive forces balance: $r_0 = 2^{1/6}\sigma \approx 1.122 \sigma > \sigma$.
2. **Spring Constant Derivation at Equilibrium:**  
   - *Error:* Differentiating $V(r)$ twice but failing to evaluate the resulting expression at $r = r_0$, leaving a function of $r$.  
   - *Correction:* Effective spring constant is a constant value: $k = \left.\frac{d^2V}{dr^2}\right|_{r=r_0} = \frac{72\epsilon}{r_0^2} = \frac{36B}{r_0^8}$.
3. **Reduced Mass vs Individual Mass in Molecular Spectroscopy:**  
   - *Error:* Using the mass of a single atom $m$ instead of the reduced mass $\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$ in rotational and vibrational formulas.  
   - *Correction:* Two bodies rotate and vibrate about their mutual center of mass. For a homonuclear diatomic molecule like $^{35}\text{Cl}_2$, $\mu = \frac{m}{2}$. For heteronuclear molecules like $\text{HCl}$, $\mu = \frac{m_{\text{H}} m_{\text{Cl}}}{m_{\text{H}} + m_{\text{Cl}}} \approx m_{\text{H}}$.
4. **Atomic Mass Unit to SI Conversion:**  
   - *Error:* Forgetting to multiply atomic masses in $\text{u}$ by $1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$ before calculating moment of inertia $I = \mu r_0^2$ or vibrational frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/\mu}$.  
   - *Correction:* Moment of inertia MUST be in $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$ (typically $\sim 10^{-45} - 10^{-47}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$) to yield transition energies in Joules.
5. **Longest Wavelength in Rotational Spectrum:**  
   - *Error:* Calculating the $J = 0 \to 1$ transition energy as $\frac{\hbar^2}{2I}(1)(2) = \frac{\hbar^2}{I}$, but confusing transition energy $\Delta E$ with the energy of a specific state.  
   - *Correction:* $\Delta E_{1\to 0} = E_1 - E_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{I}$. Because $\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E}$, the smallest energy jump ($J = 1 \to 0$) yields the **longest wavelength** $\lambda_{\max} = \frac{4\pi^2 c I}{h}$.
6. **Chlorine Doublet Origin Misconception:**  
   - *Error:* Attributing the doublet splitting in the rotational spectrum of $\text{HCl}$ to electron spin-orbit coupling or fine structure.  
   - *Correction:* The doublet in natural $\text{HCl}$ gas is purely an **isotopic effect** arising from the natural co-existence of $\text{H}^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $\text{H}^{37}\text{Cl}$ in a $3:1$ abundance ratio.
7. **Zero-Point Energy Omission:**  
   - *Error:* Assuming the lowest vibrational energy of a molecule is zero ($E_0 = 0$).  
   - *Correction:* Due to quantum uncertainty, the ground vibrational state ($v = 0$) possesses finite zero-point energy: $E_0 = \frac{1}{2}\hbar \omega_0 = \frac{1}{2}hf_0 > 0$. Molecules NEVER come to complete rest, even at absolute zero.
